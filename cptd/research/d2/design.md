## 2.2.4: Learning and behavioral outcome statements

### Mager's three-part objective (performance, condition, criterion)
- Origin: Robert F. Mager, 1962. ATD's tool says Mager devised the method in 1962 but names no book [S3]. [S14] cites the 1997 3rd ed., *Preparing Instructional Objectives: A Critical Tool in the Development of Effective Instruction*; [S16] also credits Mager with *Preparing Instructional Objectives*. The 1962 original title (*Preparing Objectives for Programmed Instruction*) is still general knowledge only.
- Tie to the exam: serves 2.2.4 (writing outcome statements). Mager's "Criterion-Referenced Instruction" is also named in 2.1.3 (shard `learn`). ATD publishes this as a design tool [S3]; not shown in the TDBoK.
- Suggested tag: named (Mager is named in 2.1.3) or extra for 2.2.4. Not tdbok.
- Kind: set. The order is not meaningful: ATD's tool numbers the elements 1 Criterion, 2 Action, 3 Condition [S3]; Mager lists Performance, Conditions, Criterion [S14]; a written objective usually reads condition, then action, then criterion.
- Items, in Mager's own terms [S14], with ATD's tool labels [S3] (gap fill: Mager's terms now sourced):
  1. Performance (ATD: "Action Statement"): what the learner must be able to do or produce. [S14] "states what a learner is expected to be able to do"; [S3] "uses an observable action verb"
  2. Conditions (ATD: "Performance Condition Statement"): the important conditions, if any, under which the performance happens; ATD says the tools, materials, data, working aids or other items the learner will have. [S14] "the important conditions (if any)"; [S3] "tools, materials, data, working aids, or other items"
  3. Criterion (ATD: "Performance Criterion Statement"): how well the learner must perform to be acceptable. [S14] "how well someone would have to perform"; [S3] "quality or quantity standards required for acceptable achievement"
- Note Mager's forms: "Conditions" is plural and "Criterion" singular in [S14].
- ATD tool's heading for the set: "Three Elements of a Learning Objective"; it calls the result a "performance-based learning objective". [S3]
- Example from the ATD tool [S3]: "Given a spreadsheet, product data, client interview data, and working aids, learners in 'The Keys to Selling Success' will write a sales proposal that includes at least three graphics and states product benefits that match client needs." By the tool's own definitions (and Mager's): condition = "Given ...", performance = "will write a sales proposal", criterion = "that includes at least three graphics ... match client needs".
- Confusables: ABCD (adds Audience; Behavior = Mager's performance; Degree = criterion). Gagné and Briggs's five-part format: Situation, Learned Capability, Object, Action, Tools and Other Constraints [S14] (page cites "Gagne and Briggs (1997)"; that year looks wrong, so don't test it). SMART objectives. "Condition" vs "Criterion" are easy to swap: condition = what the learner is given; criterion = how well.
- Variants or contested points: **the ATD tool itself prints the example with condition and criterion swapped.** Gap fill: a second, verbatim fetch confirmed it labels "Given a spreadsheet ..." as "Performance Criterion Statement" and "that includes at least three graphics ..." as "Performance Condition Statement" (the labels follow the tool's 1–2–3 numbering, while the clauses follow sentence order). The tool's definitions are correct. Authors: use the definitions, never the example's labels, and don't write a question whose key depends on the tool's example.
- Unverified: the 1962 first-edition title (general knowledge).

### ABCD objectives (Audience, Behavior, Condition, Degree)
- Origin: Heinich, Molenda, Russell and Smaldino. [S15] cites "R. Heinich, M. Molenda, J. Russell, S. Smaldino (2002)", *Instructional Media and Technologies for Learning*, 7th ed., Prentice Hall. (Gap fill: origin now sourced to a university tool citing the book. Earlier editions' title, *Instructional Media and the New Technologies of Instruction*, and the tie to the ASSURE model's "State objectives" step are still general knowledge only.) Hodell's book indexes it as the "A-B-C-D format". [S4]
- Tie to the exam: serves 2.2.4. Covered in Hodell's *ISD From the Ground Up* (2nd ed. index: audience pp. 50–51, behavior 51–52, condition 52–53, degree 53–54), and Hodell is on ATD's 2.2 reading list (5th ed.). [S1][S4]
- Suggested tag: reflist
- Kind: ordered in Hodell's index and in the acronym (A, B, C, D). The order is the mnemonic's letter order, not a required sentence order; many written ABCD objectives put the condition first.
- Items, in Hodell's index terms [S4], with [S15]'s definitions (gap fill: meanings now sourced):
  1. Audience: the group of learners the objective is written for. [S4] index entry "audience, 50–51"; [S15] "the group of learners that the objective is written for"
  2. Behavior: the verb for what the learner will be able to do after the instruction. [S4] "behavior, 51–52"; [S15] "the verb that describes what the learner (audience) will be able to do"
  3. Condition: the circumstances under which the objective must be completed. [S4] "condition, 52–53"; [S15] "the circumstances under which the objective must be completed"
  4. Degree: the standard the learner must meet for acceptable performance. [S4] "degree, 53–54"; [S15] "the standard that the learner must meet"
- Labeled example [S15]: "After participating on our 45-minute session, participants will be able to select 5 barriers to social change in their community." Condition = "After participating on our 45-minute session"; Audience = "participants"; Behavior = "select"; Degree = "5 barriers". (The condition comes first in the sentence, which shows the letter order isn't a sentence order.)
- Mapping to Mager: Behavior = Action Statement (performance); Condition = Performance Condition Statement; Degree = Performance Criterion Statement. Audience is the extra part. [S3][S4]
- Confusables: Mager's three parts (no Audience; uses "criterion" not "degree"). ASSURE (the media-planning model it comes from; see 2.2.11). Kirkpatrick Level 3 "Behavior" (Hodell's index also has "behavior (level 3 of Kirkpatrick's levels)"), which is an evaluation level, not an objective part. [S4]
- Variants or contested points: some sources write "Degree" as "Criterion" or "Standard". Hodell uses "degree". [S4]
- Unverified: the ASSURE tie and the pre-2002 edition history (general knowledge). [S14] lists the four ABCD names but attributes them to no one.

### Phillips levels of objectives (0–5) and the chain of impact
- Origin: Jack J. Phillips and Patricia Pulliam Phillips, *Beyond Learning Objectives*, © 2008, ASTD Press. [S16] (Gap fill: authors confirmed; date corrected from 2016 to 2008.) [S5] is ATD's sample of chapter 1.
- Tie to the exam: serves 2.2.4's "learning and behavioral outcome statements": the book separates learning objectives (Level 2) from application objectives that describe on-the-job action (Level 3), and links both up to impact. ATD Press, but not on the 2025 reading list as far as [S1] showed. The Phillips ROI levels also belong to shard `eval` (2.8).
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: ordered, lowest to highest level (each level must happen for the next to follow).
- Items, in the source's terms (Table 1.1, labels confirmed verbatim in [S16]; meanings from [S5]):
  0. Inputs and Indicators: project inputs such as scope, volume, efficiency and cost.
  1. Reaction and Perceived Value: how participants respond, including relevance and intent to use.
  2. Learning and Confidence: skills, knowledge and confidence gained.
  3. Application and Implementation: how the content is used on the job, including barriers and enablers.
  4. Impact and Consequences: business results such as productivity, quality and customer satisfaction.
  5. ROI: monetary benefits compared with costs (benefit-cost ratio, payback period).
- Related points from the same chapter [S5]:
  - Definition: [S5][S16] "An objective is a statement describing an intended outcome rather than a process."
  - Chain of impact: Levels 0–5 must each occur in sequence for a positive ROI.
  - Moving up the levels shifts objectives from what participants must learn to what they must do on the job. Business needs at the impact level are translated into application objectives.
  - Impact and ROI objectives should be written alongside the lower-level objectives, not afterward.
  - An objective's parts include conditions (circumstances of performance) and criteria (precise amounts, deadlines); [S16] has subsections headed "Conditions." and "Criterion." and credits Mager's *Preparing Instructional Objectives*, without presenting his model. Common problems: unclear, incomplete, nonspecific or missing objectives.
- Confusables: Kirkpatrick's four levels (no Level 0 or ROI level; shard `eval`). Phillips's five-level ROI evaluation framework (same author, evaluation side). Mager's condition and criterion.
- Variants or contested points: the table numbers six levels 0–5; elsewhere Phillips describes five levels (1–5) plus inputs. Say "Level 0, Inputs and Indicators" exactly if used. [S5]
- Unverified: none for the labels and authorship (gap fill: both confirmed in [S16]). Whether this book is on any ATD CI reading list is still unknown ([S1] didn't show it).

### Learning objectives vs behavioral (performance/application) objectives
- Origin: general instructional-design usage; the split in [S5] (Level 2 learning vs Level 3 application) and Hodell's index tie of "behavior" to Kirkpatrick Level 3 [S4] are the closest sourced versions.
- Tie to the exam: the statement itself names "learning and behavioral outcome statements" (2.2.4).
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: set (two types).
- Items:
  1. Learning outcome (learning objective): what the learner will know or be able to do by the end of the learning event (skills, knowledge, confidence). [S5]
  2. Behavioral outcome (application/performance objective): what the learner will do on the job afterward, the transfer of learning to work. [S5] application objectives define expectations as on-the-job action.
- Confusables: Kirkpatrick Level 2 (Learning) vs Level 3 (Behavior). Terminal vs enabling objectives (a different split, by scope, not by setting).
- Unverified: no tier 1–3 source fetched defines "behavioral outcome statement" in those exact words. Terminal vs enabling objectives: gap fill found only an ATD sentence that a course needs both, [S18] "Every training course should have both a terminal and an enabling learning objective", with no definition of either. [S14] and [S16] (first ~100k characters) don't mention them. The meanings (terminal = the end-of-course performance; enabling = the sub-skills that build to it) remain general knowledge: an author may say ATD expects both, but must not write a definition question from this file.

## 2.2.6: Blueprints, schematics and other visual representations

The statement's named examples are wireframes, storyboards and mockups. ATD also publishes a TD at Work issue, "Using Prototyping in Instructional Design" (td.org product 751410), and a micro-course "Storyboarding and Prototyping"; neither was opened.

### Three levels of instructional design prototypes
- Origin: ATD Blog, 2014, author not shown. [S6]
- Tie to the exam: serves 2.2.6 (wireframes are a named example; prototypes are visual representations of a solution). Only an ATD blog; not on the reading list or shown in the TDBoK.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: ordered, lowest to highest fidelity (and earliest to latest in the design).
- Items, in the source's terms [S6]:
  1. Physical (paper prototype): built from ordinary office supplies, for quick feedback from stakeholders and users in the same room. [S6] "can be built using common office supplies"
  2. Wireframe digital: built in software from libraries of common elements (drop-down menus, buttons, clickable interactions); used once options are narrowed to a few, and can be tested with remote users. [S6] "offers the next level of fidelity"
  3. Refined digital: the polished wireframe with final color, button design and placement, used once the design is narrowed to one, with working functionality for final testing. [S6] "a polished version of the wireframe prototype"
- Confusables: SAM's alpha / beta / gold releases (development stages, not prototype fidelity levels). Storyboard (a planning document, not an interactive prototype). "Mockup" is a named example in 2.2.6 but [S6] doesn't use the word.
- Variants or contested points: the general UX convention is wireframe (low fidelity, layout only) → mockup (static, high visual fidelity) → prototype (interactive). That convention is general knowledge, not from an ATD source; [S6] instead folds visual polish into "refined digital".
- Unverified: definitions of "mockup" and the wireframe/mockup/prototype ladder. Gap fill: [S19] uses "wireframe" only in passing ([S19] "a simple wireframe prototype can help you validate that your concepts are feasible"), which fits [S6]'s use of a wireframe as a prototype level, but defines neither wireframe nor mockup; "mockup" doesn't appear in [S6] or [S19]. ### Storyboard
- Origin: general design practice; ATD Blog 2015 and 2019 for the ATD framing. [S7][S19]
- Tie to the exam: named in 2.2.6.
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: set (what a storyboard combines).
- Definition: [S19] "a written outline or script of your entire e-learning course." [S7]'s definition: "the process of visual thinking and planning displayed in sequence"; [S7] also calls the storyboard the project's blueprint and a record kept for later maintenance. [S7] "The storyboard is your blueprint"
- Storyboard vs rapid prototype (gap fill) [S19]:
  1. Storyboard: a written outline or script of the whole course. Use it when stakeholders aren't familiar with e-learning development (they tend to comment on placeholder images instead of content), or when you expect many content edits (written content is easier to revise).
  2. Rapid prototype: [S19] "a rough working version of your e-learning course." Use it when stakeholders know the development process (they understand a prototype isn't finished), or when you need to validate complex interactivity such as scenarios or branching.
  - The author's conclusion: [S19] "neither method is best"; it depends on the project, developer and stakeholders.
- Items (what a storyboard should blend into one document or set of documents) [S7]:
  1. Content
  2. Assets
  3. Instruction
  4. Navigation
- Other points [S7]: keep at least two documents, one for instructional design and one for visual design; a quick start is sketching the course flow on graph paper from the learner's point of view.
- Confusables: rapid prototype (a working version, not a written outline [S19]). Design document (the high-level plan for the whole program: objectives, audience, sequence, methods, assessment), wireframe (layout of one screen), script (narration text): these three are general knowledge distinctions, not sourced here. Note that [S19] itself calls a storyboard a "script", so don't build a storyboard-vs-script question.
- Unverified: standard storyboard fields (screen number, on-screen text, narration/audio, graphics, interactions, navigation, notes) and storyboard types (text-based, visual, rapid). No ATD source fetched lists them.

## 2.2.11: Selecting and aligning delivery options and media

ATD-published but not fetched: Huggett, *The Virtual Training Guidebook* 2nd ed. sample chapter (reading list, under 2.3), Ruth Colvin Clark, *Evidence-Based Training Methods* 3rd ed. sample, and McArdle, *Training Design and Delivery* 3rd ed. sample (all on assets.td.org). An author wanting more delivery-selection content should start there.

### Synchronous vs asynchronous delivery
- Origin: general usage; ATD Blog 2021 for definitions and selection factors. [S9]
- Tie to the exam: serves 2.2.11 (the basic split among delivery options). Hodell's index also has an "asynchronous learning" entry (pp. 40, 175). [S4][S9]
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set (two types).
- Items [S9]:
  1. Synchronous: learning in real time with an instructor, where learners can interact with each other (live ILT or virtual ILT, discussion, Q&A, live practice).
  2. Asynchronous: learning that doesn't need participants and instructors engaged at the same time (on-demand courses, recordings, reference material, self-paced research).
- Selection factors the article gives (paraphrased; not a formal list) [S9]:
  1. Bloom level: lower-level content (facts, figures) suits asynchronous; higher-level content needing analysis and interaction suits synchronous.
  2. Need for interaction: if the lesson would lose much without it, go synchronous.
  3. Learner pacing and reflection time: favors asynchronous.
  4. Team belonging: synchronous builds connection.
  5. Cost: synchronous must be repeated for each group.
  6. Preparation: [S9] "Asynchronous work is a great modality for prep prior to a synchronous session."
  7. It's a mix, not either/or: [S9] "It's not an either/or training environment these days."
- Confusables: "virtual" is not the same as "asynchronous": virtual ILT is synchronous. Blended learning (a mix of modalities, see next).
- Unverified: none beyond the article being an unsigned blog post.

### Blended learning components and modality options
- Origin: ATD Blog 2015, author not shown. [S8]
- Tie to the exam: serves 2.2.11 (choosing and aligning modalities to each part of a program). Blog only.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set (components of a blended program, each with modality options).
- Items, the six components in the source's terms, with the modality options it lists [S8]:
  1. Course Content (delivering information): face-to-face instructor-led; virtual instructor-led (synchronous); recorded session (asynchronous); video; e-learning CBT; web-based resource links; interactive PDFs.
  2. Interaction (among participants, instructors and coaches or mentors): in person; interactive chat; threaded discussion; email; Skype; telephone; e-learning CBT interactivity.
  3. Practice (applying skills): live hands-on; role play; virtual lab; simulation; branched scenarios.
  4. Resources (job aids and quick references): printed; electronic; online access.
  5. Assessment (measuring learning and performance): self-checks within e-learning; formal pre- and post-testing; live demonstrated performance (authentic assessment); self-evaluation; graded assignments; instructor evaluation; peer evaluation; certification.
  6. Performance Support (reinforcing learning on the job): coach, mentor or peer mentor, in person or virtual; printed, electronic or online access.
- Selection advice [S8]: weigh the content, the audience and the resources available (including technology); risk may dictate the modality (for example compliance training); make sure self-directed work gets done (workbooks, coach review, a test); build enough interactivity into virtual ILT that participation is required; set up measures before and during the program; ask employees for feedback. [S8] "Understanding which modality to use for what type of content or training is key"
- Confusables: Kirkpatrick or Phillips levels (assessment is one component here, not a level). Role play appears both here (Practice) and as a 2.2.12 learning asset.
- Unverified: the six component names came through a fetch summary; recheck the exact headings before using them as item names. (Gap fill: not rechecked; budget went to statement-named items.)

## 2.2.12: Learning assets (role plays, self-assessments, training manuals, job aids, visual aids)

### Job aid: definition and when to use one
- Origin: ATD course tool. [S10]
- Tie to the exam: job aids are named in 2.2.12.
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: set (the four task traits that make a task a good job-aid candidate).

### Job aid design considerations
- Origin: ATD course tool. [S10]
- Tie to the exam: 2.2.12 (designing assets that align to an outcome).
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set
- Items, in the source's terms: the first seven rows of "Table 1: Tips for creating job aids" (columns "Tips" and "Details"), under the heading "Tips for Creating Job Aids". Gap fill: all row names confirmed verbatim. [S10]
  1. One task at a time: [S10] "One job aid equals one task."
  2. Keep it brief: keywords and fragments, nouns, concrete language.
  3. Design for the learner: clear heading, familiar terms, examples from the learner's job.
  4. Make it visual: icons, charts, white space, visual hierarchy.
  5. Chunk the content: separate blocks, guided by numbers, arrows or boxes.
  6. Eliminate irrelevancies: cut text or visuals that don't serve the task.
  7. Make it accessible: high contrast, shapes or bold rather than color alone, alt text, captions and audio description.
- Format-specific tips: the table's last three rows, "Merchandise", "Pop-ups" and "Digital" [S10]: merchandise (mouse mats, equipment stickers at the point of work, tested with users); pop-ups (plain, very short, concrete); digital (design for the screen; phones need bigger type, less text, captioned video). The table runs all ten rows together; splitting off these three as "format-specific" is this file's grouping, not the source's. So "how many tips" has no clean answer: don't test the count.
- Confusables: Mayer's multimedia principles (coherence ≈ eliminate irrelevancies; segmenting ≈ chunking), not sourced here.
- Unverified: none for the row names. (The tool's own title is printed "Design Considerations for Jobs Aids", a typo; cite it as "Design Considerations for Job Aids".)

### Structured vs spontaneous role playing
- Origin: Wohlking and Weiner, 1971, *Training and Development Journal*. Structured role playing is credited to Norman Maier (case method plus psychodrama); spontaneous role playing (psychodrama) to J. L. Moreno. [S11]
- Tie to the exam: role plays are named in 2.2.12. The article is in ATD's journal archive; not on the reading list or shown in the TDBoK.
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: set (two types).
- Items, in the source's terms [S11]:
  1. Structured role playing: built on a written case with separate written roles; aims at job skills (problem solving, communication), procedures and job-related attitudes. Roles are handed out just before the enactment, and the trainer interferes little.
  2. Spontaneous role playing (psychodrama): unscripted, built around participants' own problems; aims at insight into one's own and others' behavior, attitude change and new ways of handling relationships. The instructor intervenes often. [S11] "the instructor is much more active"
- Phases both types share, first to last (implied by the article's comparison headings) [S11]:
  1. Warm-up: structured needs more advance planning (focused discussion, a case, a lecture or film); spontaneous needs less planning but more facilitation skill.
  2. Enactment.
  3. Post-enactment discussion: structured critiques the interaction and principles, using observer reports; spontaneous focuses on feelings.
- Named techniques [S11]: sociodrama (a problem most of the group shares) vs psychodrama (one person's problem); multiple enactments (several groups run the same case at once, non-players observe); role rotation (different players take the same role in replays); role reversal (players swap roles); observer sheets (guides telling observers which behaviors to record); auxiliary ego or double (speaks for a player to bring out unspoken feelings).
- Confusables: case study (no enactment), simulation, branched scenario (both listed as practice options in [S8]). Sociodrama vs psychodrama. Role rotation vs role reversal.
- Variants or contested points: the article is from 1971; current ATD writing tends to describe role-play formats (pairs, triads with an observer, fishbowl) rather than this structured/spontaneous split. Those formats are general knowledge, not fetched.
- Unverified: the phase list is inferred from the article's comparison headings, not stated as a model.

### Role-play techniques
- Origin: Wohlking and Weiner, 1971. [S11]

## Process context: ADDIE and SAM (serves all four statements)

### ADDIE
- Origin: developed for the US armed forces in the 1970s (Florida State University's Interservice Procedures for Instructional Systems Development, 1975) — general knowledge, not fetched. Hodell's *ISD From the Ground Up* indexes "ADDIE model" on pp. 2 and 11–13 and the book is ADDIE-based. [S4]
- Tie to the exam: the process the four 2.2 statements sit in: outcome statements are written in Design, blueprints and storyboards are Design outputs, delivery and media are chosen in Design, and assets are built in Development. On ATD's reading list via Hodell. [S1][S4]
- Suggested tag: reflist
- Kind: ordered, first phase to last.
- Items (standard names; only the acronym's index entry was seen in [S4]):
  1. Analysis: identify the need, audience, tasks and constraints.
  2. Design: write objectives, plan assessments, sequence, strategies, delivery and media; produce the design document.
  3. Development: build and test the materials and assets.
  4. Implementation: deliver the program (including train-the-trainer and logistics).
  5. Evaluation: formative evaluation throughout and summative evaluation after.
- Confusables: SAM (iterative alternative). Dick and Carey's systems model (ten steps; not sourced here). Some sources write the phases as "Analyze, Design, Develop, Implement, Evaluate".
- Variants or contested points: the origin story (FSU 1975 vs the acronym appearing later) is debated; don't test it.
- Unverified: phase definitions and origin (general knowledge only).

### SAM (Successive Approximation Model)
- Origin: Michael Allen, Allen Interactions; *Leaving ADDIE for SAM* (Allen with Richard Sites, ASTD Press, 2012; title and coauthor are general knowledge, an O'Reilly copy appeared in search results). The poster is © 2015 Allen Interactions and ATD. [S13]
- Tie to the exam: iterative design-and-prototype process for 2.2.6 (rapid prototypes replace specification documents) and 2.2.12. Not on the 2025 reading list as far as [S1] showed.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: ordered, first to last (the version on the poster, SAM2 by structure).
- Phases, in the model owner's terms [S17] (gap fill: corrected; the Savvy Start moved from Preparation to Iterative Design, and the cycles are now sourced):
  1. Preparation: Information Gathering [S13].
  2. Iterative Design: [S17] "This phase begins with the Savvy Start" (a kickoff where key stakeholders brainstorm and prototype together [S13]), followed by Project Planning and Additional Design [S13]. The team rotates through [S17] "design, prototype, and review."
  3. Iterative Development: Design Proof, then Alpha, Beta and Gold. The team works [S17] "through development, implementation, and evaluation together."
- Rollout: [S13]'s poster shows it, but [S17] names only the three phases above. Don't count Rollout as a fourth phase in a question.
- Named releases, first to last [S13][S17]:
  1. Design Proof: shows all design decisions together, to confirm direction before content is built at scale.
  2. Alpha: early release usable enough for evaluation.
  3. Beta: the first release candidate.
  4. Gold: the final release (reached early if the Beta has no problems).
- Other definitions [S13]: rapid prototypes are quick working models used in place of specification documents; iterative design works in layers, covering all content types before refining any in depth.
- Confusables: the three ID prototype levels in [S6] (fidelity levels, not releases). ADDIE's Development and Evaluation phases. SAM1 (a simpler three-step loop: evaluate, design, develop, for small projects).
- Variants or contested points: SAM1 vs SAM2 ([S17] mentions neither). Drawings differ on where the Savvy Start sits (the earlier fetch of the poster [S13] placed it after Information Gathering in Preparation). Allen's own page [S17] puts it at the start of Iterative Design: use that, and avoid a question that hinges on the boundary. Spelling: [S17] writes "Savvy Start" (both words capitalized, no hyphen); the poster writes "SAVVY Start".
- Unverified: SAM1's steps; the *Leaving ADDIE for SAM* details. (Gap fill: the cycle-to-phase mapping is now sourced to [S17].)
