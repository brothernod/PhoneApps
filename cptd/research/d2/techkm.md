## 2.4.1: Selecting, integrating, managing, and maintaining learning platforms

Statement examples to cover: learning management systems, knowledge management systems, performance management systems.

### Foreman's five steps to evaluate and select an LMS
- Origin: Steve Foreman, Learning Guild article, 2013 [S12]. He is the author of *The LMS Guidebook* (ATD, 2017), whose Chapter 6 is "Selecting the Right LMS for Your Organization" [S11].
- Tie to the exam: 2.4.1 ("selecting ... learning platforms") and 2.4.4 ("using evaluative criteria"). The author's book is on ATD's 2025 reading list under 2.4 [S1]. The five steps themselves come from his 2013 article, not from the book page, so whether the book uses the same five is unverified.
- Suggested tag: reflist (author on the list), but word it as "Foreman recommends"; don't claim the steps are in the book
- Kind: ordered (the sequence of the selection project, first to last)
- Items, in the source's own terms [S12]:
  1. Needs analysis: interview leaders and stakeholders to find the strategic, operational and technical drivers.
  2. Requirements definition: write clear, separate requirements, each stating a need rather than a solution, then prioritize them. Foreman uses the analytic hierarchy process for prioritizing. "Each requirement should express a need, not a solution."
  3. Product vetting: cut the market to a short list with a few knockout criteria, such as multi-language support, accessibility and security compliance, and ballpark license cost. He suggests about eight to ten distinctive requirements that rule out products that don't qualify.
  4. Product evaluation: filter the short list, funnel-style, through an RFI (requirements with open-ended questions), use-case demonstrations (scenarios given to vendors in advance), sandbox trials, customer references, and financial-health checks (public filings, Dun & Bradstreet, analysts). "The product evaluation process works like a funnel."
  5. Product selection: pick two or three finalists, send them an RFP (pricing, implementation timeline, support options), and negotiate price and terms.
- Evaluative criteria named [S12]: drivers (strategic, operational, technical). Requirement categories (functional, technical, cost). Scoring with a weighted scorecard based on requirement priority, a five-point rating scale for RFI answers and demos, and scores per requirement, per category and overall.
- Confusables: RFI vs RFP. Foreman sends the RFI to the short list and the RFP only to the two or three finalists, so a question that reverses them is wrong. "Needs analysis" (step 1) vs needs assessment for training (Domain 3 or the `design` shard). The Atwood KM process also starts with needs.
- Variants or contested points: The article doesn't use "must-have vs nice-to-have", though the book blurb mentions "must-have features" [S11]. Many vendor guides order things differently (RFP first). Use Foreman's order.
- Unverified: whether Chapter 6 of the book gives the same five steps under the same names.

### Learning platform types (named in 2.4.1, plus close relatives)
- Origin: The statement names three types (LMS, KMS, performance management systems). The others come from Foreman [S11] (LCMS, corporate vs academic LMS), an ATD blog article [S13] (LMS vs LXP) and general TD usage.
- Tie to the exam: named in the statement (LMS, KMS, PMS). The LCMS appears in a reading-list book's chapter title [S11].
- Suggested tag: named (LMS, KMS, PMS); reflist (LCMS, corporate/academic LMS categories); extra (LXP, talent management suite)
- Kind: set
- Items:
  1. Learning management system (LMS): in ATD's framing, a system for organizing and delivering the content the organization decides its people must know, assigned by department, job function or similar criteria. The model is top-down and organization-driven. [S13] "focus on organizing and delivering the topics an organization identifies as critical". Foreman divides LMS features into Corporate and Academic. [S11] The administer/track/report functions (enrollments, completions, compliance records) are general knowledge, not in [S13]; the book's own definition (Ch. 1) was not seen.
  2. Learning content management system (LCMS): a system for authoring, storing, reusing and managing learning content (often as reusable objects), sometimes combined with an LMS ("LCMS-LMS"). [S11] for the term; definition from general knowledge.
  3. Knowledge management system (KMS): a system that captures, organizes, stores and lets people find organizational knowledge (repositories, wikis, expert directories, lessons-learned databases). Expert directories also appear in the personalization strategy [S3]; repositories in codification [S3]. Definition from general knowledge.
  4. Performance management system: a system for setting goals, tracking progress, and recording reviews and feedback. It is often part of a talent management suite and linked to the LMS so development plans can link to learning. General knowledge only.
  5. Learning experience platform (LXP): a learner-centered platform that identifies and personalizes growth opportunities for each employee. It uses AI to build individual learning paths from self-reported data, data on similar users and data on the learner. It draws on varied content, including material curated from the internet, and puts the employee in charge of their own development. [S13] "focus on the learner journey by identifying and personalizing opportunities". It is relevant to curation (2.5.7) too. (The "LEP" abbreviation is from memory; [S13] uses LXP.)
- Confusables: LMS vs LCMS (track and administer vs author and manage content). LXP vs LMS: learner-driven personalization vs organization-driven delivery [S13]. ATD notes the line is blurring, because some LMS vendors now offer LXP-like features, so check what a system actually does [S13]. Don't write a question that treats the two as cleanly separate product categories. KMS vs LCMS (organizational knowledge vs learning content). PMS (performance management) vs EPSS (electronic performance support system, Gery), which is in-the-flow job support, not appraisal.
- Unverified: the LCMS, KMS and performance management system definitions (general knowledge only; [S13] doesn't define them and no other ATD source was found within budget). LMS and LXP are now sourced to an ATD article [S13], not the TDBoK. Authors: for LMS vs LXP, use the [S13] contrast (organization-directed delivery vs learner-centered personalization). For LCMS, KMS and PMS, keep questions at the level of "track/administer vs create/manage content" and get a checker to confirm.

### Modern Learning Ecosystem (MLE) Framework
- Origin: JD Dillon. First published as a blog framework (2017) [S9]. Book: *The Modern Learning Ecosystem: A New L&D Mindset for the Ever-Changing Workplace*, ATD Press, 2022 [S10].
- Tie to the exam: 2.4.4 and 2.4.1. The book is on ATD's 2025 reading list under capability 2.4 [S1] (and under 3.8). It matches "identifying appropriate applications": it says which kind of support or technology fits which need.
- Suggested tag: reflist
- Kind: ordered, bottom to top. Layer 1 is the base. You start at the bottom and go only as high as the problem needs, so formal ("push") training is the last resort. [S9]
- Items, using the 2022 book names [S10], with the 2017 names in brackets [S9]:
  1. Shared knowledge [2017: same]: searchable, easy-to-find reference information. It is the foundation of the framework. [S9] "shared knowledge is the foundation on which everything else is based"
  2. Performance support [same]: help available in the workflow, a way for employees to signal that they need help and get it at the moment of need. [S9]
  3. Reinforcement [2017: Continued reinforcement]: keeping must-know information fresh over time so it is retained. [S9]
  4. Coaching [2017: Management support]: equipping managers to coach the behaviors involved. [S9]
  5. Pull training [2017: On-demand training]: structured learning that employees choose and pull when they need it. [S9]
  6. Push training [2017: Formal training]: assigned or required training, used only when necessary. [S9] "Push training content only when necessary"
- Related rules: Installing the framework needs three components: framework, tactics and process. [S9] Tactics are the right-fit tools in each layer. Traditional methods such as ILT are slotted into a layer, not removed. [S9] Dillon's process "big rule" is to build no instructional material on a topic until its information is shared in a searchable way. [S9] The ATD release calls the six layers "six elements all employees need for doing their best work". [S10]
- Confusables: Mosher & Gottfredson's Five Moments of Need and the 70-20-10 model, both of which Dillon cites as inspirations [S9]. Gery's performance support (EPSS). The learning ecosystem component lists in Udall & Woodill. "Pull vs push" is also used for knowledge flows in KM and in Jarche's Seek (pull, pushed by trusted sources) [S8].
- Variants or contested points: The layer names changed between 2017 and 2022 (see brackets). Use the 2022 names; they come from the reading-list book's own publisher [S10]. That the 2022 order matches the 2017 bottom-to-top numbering is my inference from the same order in both sources. The release lists them in that order but doesn't say "bottom to top".
- Unverified: whether the 2022 book still keeps the "framework, tactics, process" trio and the "big rule", and its full measurement chapter.

### Technology adoption and integration models (SAMR sourced; Rogers and TAM UNSOURCED)
These three are common answers to "how do I judge whether a learning technology fits and will be adopted". They serve 2.4.4 ("identifying appropriate applications in an instructional environment" and "implementing"). The gap fill sourced SAMR to its author [S20]. #### SAMR model
- Origin: Ruben R. Puentedura (hippasus.com). The "Two Questions" handout used here is filed under 2011 [S20]. "About 2006" for the model's first appearance is from memory; don't test the date.
- Tie to the exam: 2.4.4, "appropriate applications in an instructional environment". No ATD tie shown.
- Kind: ordered (lowest to highest degree of change the technology brings to the task; Puentedura asks what must be added to move a task from S to A to M to R [S20])
- Items, in the source's own terms [S20]:
  1. Substitution: tech is a direct substitute for the old tool, with no functional change. [S20] "Tech acts as a direct tool substitute, with no functional change"
  2. Augmentation: tech is still a direct substitute, but with functional improvement. [S20]
  3. Modification: tech allows significant task redesign. [S20]
  4. Redefinition: tech allows the creation of new tasks that were previously inconceivable. [S20] "Tech allows for the creation of new tasks, previously inconceivable"
- Groups: Enhancement and Transformation are both labels on the handout [S20]. The fetch reported that the layout places Enhancement beside the first two levels and Transformation beside the last two, but the text doesn't state it. So Enhancement = Substitution + Augmentation and Transformation = Modification + Redefinition is a layout reading, consistent with common usage.
- Changed: Redefinition now uses the source's word "inconceivable" (was "impossible").
- Confusables: TPACK (Mishra & Koehler 2006; knowledge domains, not levels). Bloom's levels. The trap pair is Substitution vs Augmentation: both are direct substitutes, and only Augmentation adds functional improvement.
- Unverified: the origin year, and the Enhancement/Transformation grouping as a stated rule (it is shown only by layout).

- Items:
  1. Start from needs and drivers (strategic, operational, technical), not from the tool. [S12]
  2. State requirements as needs, not solutions, and prioritize them. [S12]
  3. Use evaluative criteria in categories: functional, technical, cost. Use knockout criteria such as multi-language support, accessibility and security compliance, and license cost. [S12]
  4. Test fit with real use cases (scripted demos, sandbox trials), references and vendor financial health. [S12]
  5. Choose the right-fit tactic or tool for each layer of the learning ecosystem, starting from shared knowledge and performance support before formal training. [S9][S10]
  6. Plan the implementation, configure, integrate with other systems, migrate data, test, go live, then operate under standards and governance. [S11]
- Unverified: whether the TDBoK gives its own list of technology selection criteria (for example cost, scalability, security, accessibility, ease of use, vendor support, integration). That list is typical but not shown here.

## 2.5.3: Knowledge management strategy

### Tacit vs explicit knowledge
- Origin: The tacit idea is Michael Polanyi's (*Personal Knowledge*, 1958 [S21] "attributed to Michael Polanyi in 1958 in Personal Knowledge"; *The Tacit Dimension*, 1966, general knowledge, not fetched). The KM form used in TD comes from Nonaka & Takeuchi (*The Knowledge-Creating Company*, 1995) and Nonaka, Toyama & Konno (2000). [S2]
- Tie to the exam: 2.5.3 (KM strategy). It's the base distinction behind every KM strategy and behind SECI. It is in the scope plan's expected model load. Not shown on the reading list by name (the list gives titles only). The TDBoK text isn't available, so the TDBoK tie can't be shown.
- Suggested tag: extra (raise to reflist only if an author can show it in Atwood's *Knowledge Management Basics* [S1])
- Kind: set (two types)
- Items, in the source's own terms:
  1. Explicit knowledge: knowledge that can be put into words, numbers and formal systems, and shared as data, formulas or manuals. It is easy to process, transmit and store. [S2]
  2. Tacit knowledge: personal know-how rooted in action, routines, commitment, values and emotions. It is hard to put into words or pass on. [S2] "highly personal and hard to formalise"
- Confusables: codification vs personalization (strategy choice, not knowledge types). Data/information/knowledge (DIKW). "Implicit" knowledge, a third category some KM writers add (knowledge that could be made explicit but hasn't been). Don't treat it as part of Nonaka's pair.
- Variants or contested points: Polanyi's "we can know more than we can tell" is the classic phrasing (general knowledge). British spelling in [S2] ("formalise", "socialisation"). Use US spelling in the app unless quoting.
- Unverified: TDBoK wording of these definitions.

### SECI model (knowledge conversion / knowledge spiral)
- Origin: Ikujiro Nonaka & Hirotaka Takeuchi, *The Knowledge-Creating Company*, 1995 (first form in Nonaka 1991/1994). Restated in Nonaka, Toyama & Konno, 2000. [S2]
- Tie to the exam: 2.5.3. The scope plan names it as expected KM load. It's the best-known KM model, but the tie is not shown in a tier 1 or 2 source.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: ordered (a cycle; the source order is S → E → C → I, then a new spiral)
- Items, in the source's own terms:
  1. Socialisation (tacit → tacit): sharing tacit knowledge through shared experience, such as spending time together, apprenticeship, or on-the-job observation. [S2]
  2. Externalisation (tacit → explicit): putting tacit knowledge into words, concepts, metaphors or models so that others can use it. [S2]
  3. Combination (explicit → explicit): gathering, sorting and joining pieces of explicit knowledge into more complex systems, such as documents, databases or reports. [S2]
  4. Internalisation (explicit → tacit): learning by doing. A person absorbs explicit knowledge, such as manuals or simulations, until it becomes their own know-how. [S2]
- Groups: The first letter of each mode spells SECI. Each mode is one conversion between the two knowledge types.
- Confusables: The four types of ba (each pairs with one mode). Kolb's experiential cycle (also four steps in a loop; covered in the `learn` shard). Tuckman's stages. Exam traps are the direction of each conversion, especially Externalisation (tacit → explicit) vs Internalisation (explicit → tacit).
- Variants or contested points: The source says knowledge creation is a spiral, not a circle. It grows as it moves from individual to group to organization and beyond. [S2] "forms a spiral, not a circle." Some critics (for example Gourlay, Kingston eprint in search results, not fetched) dispute whether tacit knowledge can truly be converted. That debate is not likely to be tested.
- Unverified: whether the TDBoK or Atwood [S1] present SECI, and in what spelling.

### Ba (shared context for knowledge creation)
- Origin: Nonaka & Konno, "The Concept of 'Ba'," *California Management Review*, 1998 (not fetched). Restated with these names in Nonaka, Toyama & Konno, 2000. [S2]
- Tie to the exam: 2.5.3, as an extension of SECI. No tier 1 or 2 tie shown. Low priority; include only as an advanced item or a confusable.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set of four, mapped one-to-one to the SECI modes, on two dimensions: type of interaction (individual or collective) and medium (face-to-face or virtual). [S2]
- Items, in the source's own terms:
  1. Originating ba: individual, face-to-face. Pairs with Socialisation. [S2]
  2. Dialoguing ba: collective, face-to-face. Pairs with Externalisation. [S2]
  3. Systemising ba: collective, virtual. Pairs with Combination. [S2]
  4. Exercising ba: individual, virtual. Pairs with Internalisation. [S2]
- Confusables: The SECI modes themselves.
- Variants or contested points: The 1998 paper used different names, "interacting ba" and "cyber ba", for the second and third (from memory; not verified here). Use the 2000 names above if this is authored.
- Unverified: the 1998 names.

### Knowledge assets (four categories)
- Origin: Nonaka, Toyama & Konno, 2000. [S2]
- Tie to the exam: 2.5.3, weakly (what a KM strategy manages). No tier 1 or 2 tie shown.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set
- Items, in the source's own terms:
  1. Experiential knowledge assets: shared tacit knowledge built through hands-on experience. [S2]
  2. Conceptual knowledge assets: explicit knowledge put into images, symbols and language, such as concepts and designs. [S2]
  3. Systemic knowledge assets: systematized, packaged explicit knowledge, such as documents, manuals and databases. [S2]
  4. Routine knowledge assets: tacit knowledge built into everyday actions, routines and organizational culture. [S2]
- Confusables: the SECI modes and the types of ba (also four-item lists from the same paper).
- Unverified: the one-line meanings are paraphrased from general knowledge of the paper. The fetch confirmed the four names only. Low priority; an author may skip this model.

### Codification vs personalization (KM strategy choice)
- Origin: Morten T. Hansen, Nitin Nohria & Thomas Tierney, "What's Your Strategy for Managing Knowledge?" *Harvard Business Review*, March–April 1999, pp. 106–116 (byline in that order). [S15] Excerpt also at HBS Working Knowledge [S3].
- Tie to the exam: 2.5.3 ("designing ... knowledge management strategy"). It is the classic named pair of KM strategies. The tie is not shown in a tier 1 or 2 source.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set (two strategies; pick one as primary)
- Items, in the source's own terms:
  1. Codification strategy: knowledge is carefully codified and stored in databases, where anyone in the firm can find and reuse it. The approach is "people-to-documents". Examples: Andersen Consulting, Ernst & Young. It relies on electronic repositories and staff who codify and manage content (E&Y had a 250-person Center for Business Knowledge). [S3][S15] "people-to-documents"
  2. Personalization strategy: knowledge is closely tied to the person who developed it and is shared mainly through direct contact. IT is used to find who knows what (expert directories), not to store knowledge objects. Examples in the article's exhibit: McKinsey & Company and Bain & Company [S15]. ([S3] was earlier read as also naming BCG; the exhibit names only McKinsey and Bain, so use those two.) It relies on people networks, transfers between offices, and returning colleagues' calls quickly. [S3][S15]
- Choosing the primary strategy [S23]: "Do your people rely on explicit or tacit knowledge to solve problems?" "When people use tacit knowledge most often to solve problems, the person-to-person approach works best."
- Key principle (80/20): Don't split effort evenly. Effective firms focus on one strategy and use the other in a supporting role. [S3] The authors call this an 80-20 split. [S15] "80% of their knowledge sharing follows one strategy, 20% the other"
- Exhibit "How Consulting Firms Manage Their Knowledge": five comparison rows, in this order [S15]:
  1. Competitive Strategy.
  2. Economic Model: codification = Reuse economics, which means investing once in a knowledge asset and reusing it many times. [S15] "Invest once in a knowledge asset; reuse it many times." Personalization = Expert economics, which means charging high fees for highly customized solutions to unique problems. [S15]
  3. Knowledge Management Strategy: codification is people-to-documents [S3]; personalization is person-to-person [S23] "PERSON-TO-PERSON".
  4. Information Technology: heavy IT support is critical for codification and much less important for personalization. [S15]
  5. Human Resources: codification hires new college graduates suited to reusing knowledge and trains people in groups and through computer-based distance learning. Personalization hires MBAs who like problem solving and can tolerate ambiguity and trains people through one-on-one mentoring. [S15]
- Incentives (row not shown in the fetch): codification rewards people for using and contributing to document databases; personalization rewards people for directly sharing knowledge with others. [S15] At Bain, direct help given to colleagues can count for up to a quarter of a partner's annual pay. [S15]
- Confusables: Tacit vs explicit. Codification goes with explicit knowledge and personalization with tacit, but they are strategies, not knowledge types. SECI Externalisation (tacit → explicit) is a conversion, not a strategy. People also mix this up with "know-how vs know-who". Trap pairs from the exhibit: reuse vs expert economics, group/distance training vs one-on-one mentoring, new graduates vs MBAs.
- Variants or contested points: The 80/20 split is now confirmed in the full article [S15]; the HBS excerpt [S3] gives no ratio. KMWorld (search result, not fetched) calls the pair a "false dichotomy", which is a criticism, not ATD's position.
- Changed: 80/20, reuse/expert economics, the incentive differences, and the IT and HR differences moved from Unverified to confirmed [S15]. The personalization examples were cut to McKinsey and Bain (the exhibit's two). Byline and issue confirmed.
- Unverified: which exhibit row the incentives sit in (the fetch quoted the reward lines but not their row). Quotes come from an OCR copy that the fetch tool corrected, so recheck punctuation before any verbatim use.

  2. Identify sources of information (Ch. 4, "Locating Information Sources in Your Organization").

#### Communities of practice (CoP)

### Bhargava's five models of content curation
- Origin: Rohit Bhargava, "The 5 Models of Content Curation," blog post, March 31, 2011. [S7] (He also wrote the 2009 "Manifesto for the Content Curator", mentioned in the post.)
- Tie to the exam: 2.5.7 (curating ... assembling content). It's widely cited in L&D curation writing. The tie is not shown in a tier 1 or 2 source.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set (five models; the post numbers them, but there is no ranking or sequence)
- Items, in the source's own terms (order as in the post):
  1. Aggregation: collecting the most relevant information about a topic in one place. [S7] "the act of curating the most relevant information"
  2. Distillation: boiling information down to a simpler form that keeps only the most important ideas. [S7]
  3. Elevation: curating to find a bigger trend or insight hidden in many small, everyday posts. [S7] "with a mission of identifying a larger trend"
  4. Mashup: placing existing content side by side to create a new point of view. [S7] (The source's sentence contains a typo, "justapositions"; don't quote it.)

### Seek > Sense > Share (Personal Knowledge Mastery, PKM)
- Origin: Harold Jarche. The framework has appeared in his writing since the early 2010s; the post used here is from 2014. [S8]
- Tie to the exam: 2.5.7 (researching, evaluating, selecting and sharing content), and 2.5.3 at the individual level. It's the best-known curation and PKM process in L&D. The tie is not shown in a tier 1 or 2 source.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: ordered (a continuous process: Seek → Sense → Share, then repeat)
- Items, in the source's own terms:
  1. Seek: finding things out and keeping current, by building a network you can "pull" information from and by having trusted sources "push" it to you. [S8] "Seeking is finding things out and keeping up to date."
  2. Sense: making information personal and using it, through reflection, putting it into practice and experimenting. [S8] "Sensing is how we personalize information and use it."
  3. Share: exchanging resources, ideas and experiences with your networks and colleagues, and judging what to share, when and with whom. [S8]
- Acronym: PKM = Personal Knowledge Mastery in Jarche's usage. [S8] Elsewhere PKM usually means "personal knowledge management". Jarche's own term is now "mastery"; he has also written [S22] "personal knowledge management/mastery".
- Process: Jarche calls PKM a continuous process. Ideas come from social networks, are filtered through communities of practice, are made sense of by doing, and are shared with the work team and then wider networks. [S8]
- Confusables: Bhargava's five models (types, not steps). The SECI modes. Curation "find, filter, share" lists in study decks. Note Jarche's link to communities of practice.
- Variants or contested points: Jarche's post uses "Sense" and "sense-making". The step is "Sense", not "Make sense" or "Synthesize". Jarche cites Dave Snowden's maxim that we always know more than we can say [S8]. That is Snowden, not Polanyi, though it echoes Polanyi's tacit-knowledge idea.
- Unverified: whether Jarche contributed to *Ready, Set, Curate* or is cited in it or the TDBoK.
