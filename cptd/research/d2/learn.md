## 2.1.1: Foundational learning theories (behaviorism, cognitivism, constructivism)

### The three foundational learning theories (behaviorism, cognitivism, constructivism) as a set
- Origin: the umbrella grouping used in instructional design; the classic comparison is Ertmer & Newby 1993. [S2]
- Tie to the exam: named in 2.1.1 (all three named).
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: set. Ertmer & Newby present them as a rough continuum from low to high processing demands / novice to expert (behaviorism → cognitivism → constructivism), not as a strict ranking. [S2]
- Items:
  1. Behaviorism: learning is a change in the form or frequency of observable performance, built by stimulus–response associations strengthened by reinforcement; the learner reacts to the environment. [S2]
  2. Cognitivism: learning is a change between knowledge states; information is received, organized, stored and retrieved; the learner is an active processor. [S2]
  3. Constructivism: learners build their own meaning from experience; described by Ertmer & Newby as a branch of cognitivism. [S2] "creating meaning from experience"
- Confusables: social learning theory (Bandura) is often called a bridge between behaviorism and cognitivism and could be offered as a fourth "foundational" option; it is not one of the three named in 2.1.1. Humanism (Maslow, Rogers) and connectivism (Siemens) are other "learning theory" families sometimes listed beside the three. Constructivism vs social constructivism (Vygotsky) vs constructionism (Papert) are distinct terms.
- Variants or contested points: Ertmer & Newby argue no theory is "best"; they recommend "systematic eclecticism," matching the theory to the task and learner level. [S2] "Which theory is the most effective in fostering mastery of specific tasks by specific learners?"
- Unverified: TDBoK wording for 2.1.1 itself (TDBoK chapter not public).

### Ertmer & Newby's comparison questions (Schunk's five definitive questions, plus two)
- Origin: Schunk 1991 (five questions); Ertmer & Newby 1993 added two for instructional design. [S2]
- Tie to the exam: 2.1.1, general knowledge (useful for "which theory says X about memory/transfer" questions).
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set (the order is just the article's order)
- Items:
  1. How does learning occur? [S2]
  2. Which factors influence learning? [S2]
  3. What is the role of memory? [S2]
  4. How does transfer occur? [S2]
  5. What types of learning are best explained by the theory? [S2]
  6. (added) What basic assumptions/principles are relevant to instructional design? [S2]
  7. (added) How should instruction be structured? [S2]
- Shared definition of learning they start from (Shuell, via Schunk): an enduring change in behavior, or in the capacity to behave, resulting from practice or other experience. [S2] "enduring change in behavior"

### Behaviorism: key features
- Origin: Skinner (operant conditioning, teaching machines, programmed instruction) is the theorist Ertmer & Newby cite; Pavlov (classical conditioning), Thorndike (law of effect) and Watson are the other standard names. [S2] for Skinner; others see Unverified.
- Tie to the exam: named in 2.1.1.
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: set
- Items (Ertmer & Newby's answers to the questions above):
  1. How learning occurs: a stimulus produces a response; the S–R association is made, strengthened and maintained; reinforced responses are more likely to recur. [S2]
  2. Factors: learner and environment, but the environment is emphasized (arrangement of stimuli and consequences). [S2]
  3. Memory: largely not addressed; forgetting comes from nonuse of a response over time, so periodic practice and review matter. [S2]
  4. Transfer: by generalization; situations with identical or similar features allow transfer. [S2]
  5. Learning best explained: discriminations (recalling facts), generalizations (defining and illustrating concepts), associations (applying explanations), chaining (automatically performing a procedure); not adequate for higher-level skills like problem solving. [S2]
  6. ID principles: observable, measurable outcomes (behavioral objectives, task analysis, criterion-referenced assessment); pre-assessment of learners; mastery of early steps before moving on (sequencing, mastery learning); reinforcement (rewards, informative feedback); cues, shaping and practice. [S2]
  7. Structuring instruction: present a target stimulus, give cues/prompts and practice, reinforce correct responses. [S2]
- Confusables: Mager's criterion-referenced instruction and behavioral objectives grow out of behaviorism (Ertmer & Newby list behavioral objectives and criterion-referenced assessment as behaviorist ID principles). Programmed instruction and computer-assisted instruction (CAI) are behaviorist applications. [S2]
- Variants or contested points: none found.
- Unverified: Pavlov / classical conditioning, Thorndike / law of effect (and laws of readiness and exercise), Watson, and reinforcement schedules are standard textbook content but were not confirmed in a tier 1–3 source in this file (Ertmer & Newby don't mention Thorndike or Pavlov).

### Cognitivism: key features
- Origin: cognitive psychology / information processing; theorists cited by Ertmer & Newby include Bower & Hilgard, Merrill, Shuell, Winne. [S2]
- Tie to the exam: named in 2.1.1.
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: set
- Items:
  1. How learning occurs: a discrete change between states of knowledge; focus on how information is received, organized, stored and retrieved; the learner actively codes, structures and plans. [S2]
  2. Factors: environmental conditions (explanations, examples and non-examples, practice with corrective feedback) plus learner thoughts, beliefs, attitudes and values. [S2]
  3. Memory: central; learning happens when information is stored in an organized, meaningful way; forgetting is failure to retrieve (interference, memory loss, missing cues). [S2]
  4. Transfer: depends on how information is stored in memory; learner must understand how to apply knowledge in different contexts. [S2]
  5. Learning best explained: complex forms such as reasoning, problem solving, information processing. [S2]
  6. ID principles: active learner involvement (learner control, metacognitive training); hierarchical analyses of prerequisites (cognitive task analysis); structuring, organizing and sequencing information (outlines, summaries, advance organizers); linking to prior knowledge (recall of prerequisites, examples, analogies). [S2]
  7. Structuring instruction: make it meaningful by linking to existing mental structures (schemas) using analogies, metaphors, framing, outlining, mnemonics, concept mapping, advance organizers. [S2]
- Confusables: Gagné's Nine Events are grounded in information processing (cognitivist); Bloom's cognitive domain is a taxonomy of objectives, not a learning theory.
- Variants or contested points: none found.
- Unverified: the specific information-processing (multi-store) model components (sensory memory, short-term/working memory, long-term memory) and cognitive load theory were not confirmed in a tier 1–3 source here.

### Constructivism: key features
- Origin: roots in Piaget, Bruner, Goodman; situated cognition (Brown, Collins & Duguid); cognitive flexibility (Spiro); Jonassen. [S2]
- Tie to the exam: named in 2.1.1.
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: set
- Items:
  1. How learning occurs: learners create meaning from experience; knowledge is not transferred from outside but built through personal interpretation; no single "correct" meaning. [S2] "creating meaning from experience"
  2. Factors: the interaction of learner and environment; knowledge is tied to the activity and context in which it is learned. [S2]
  3. Memory: always under construction, a cumulative history of interactions; prior knowledge is assembled flexibly for the problem at hand. [S2]
  4. Transfer: through authentic tasks anchored in meaningful contexts; decontextualized learning rarely transfers. [S2]
  5. Learning best explained: advanced knowledge acquisition in ill-structured domains; Jonassen's stages (introductory, advanced, expert) suggest objectivist approaches for introductory learning and constructivist ones as learners advance. [S2]
  6. ID principles: anchor in the real-world context where skills will be used; learner control and manipulation of information; present information in varied ways and from multiple perspectives; support problem solving that goes "beyond the information given"; assess transfer with new problems. [S2]
  7. Structuring instruction: situated learning, cognitive apprenticeship (modeling and coaching), multiple perspectives, social negotiation, reflective awareness. [S2]
- Confusables: experiential learning (Kolb, 2.1.3) and social/collaborative learning overlap heavily with constructivist practice; questions should name the theory, not just "learning by doing."
- Variants or contested points: Ertmer & Newby treat constructivism as a branch of cognitivism; others treat it as a separate paradigm. [S2]
- Unverified: Vygotsky (zone of proximal development, scaffolding) as a constructivist source is standard but not in [S2]'s list as fetched; not confirmed here.

### Matching theory to task and learner level (Ertmer & Newby)
- Origin: Ertmer & Newby 1993, drawing on Schön and Jonassen. [S2]
- Tie to the exam: 2.1.1, general knowledge.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: ordered (low to high processing demand / novice to expert)
- Items:
  1. Behaviorist strategies: low processing tasks (paired associations, discriminations, rote memorization); "knowing what." [S2]
  2. Cognitivist strategies: increased processing (classifications, rule or procedure execution); "knowing how" (applying rules in new situations). [S2]
  3. Constructivist strategies: high processing (heuristic problem solving, personal selection and monitoring of strategies); "reflection-in-action" on ill-defined problems. [S2]
- Confusables: Bloom's levels (also a low-to-high ordering, but of objectives).
- Variants or contested points: none.
- Unverified: none beyond the general note above.

### Bloom's Taxonomy, original (cognitive domain, 1956)
- Origin: Bloom, B. S. (ed.), with Engelhart, Furst, Hill and Krathwohl, Taxonomy of Educational Objectives, Handbook I: Cognitive Domain, 1956. [S3]
- Tie to the exam: named in 2.1.3 ("Bloom's Taxonomy").
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: ordered, simplest/most concrete to most complex/abstract; the original was a cumulative hierarchy (each level requires mastery of the one below). [S3]
- Items (nouns, in order):
  1. Knowledge: recall of specifics, ways and means of dealing with specifics, and universals/abstractions (terminology, facts, conventions, classifications, criteria, methodology, principles, theories). [S3]
  2. Comprehension: subcategories translation, interpretation, extrapolation. [S3]
  3. Application: no subcategories. [S3]
  4. Analysis: of elements, of relationships, of organizational principles. [S3]
  5. Synthesis: producing a unique communication, a plan or proposed set of operations, or a set of abstract relations. [S3]
  6. Evaluation: judgments in terms of internal evidence and of external criteria. [S3]
- Confusables: the revised taxonomy's verbs (below); Gagné's five learning outcomes (also a taxonomy of what is learned); Ertmer & Newby's processing levels.
- Variants or contested points: see the revised version. ATD/TDBoK wording on which version it uses was not confirmed; the deck [S4] uses "Bloom's taxonomy" with domains.
- Unverified: whether the TDBoK presents the original, the revised, or both.

### Bloom's Taxonomy, revised (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001): Cognitive Process dimension
- Origin: Anderson, L. W., & Krathwohl, D. R. (eds.), with Airasian, Cruikshank, Mayer, Pintrich, Raths, Wittrock, A Taxonomy for Learning, Teaching, and Assessing, 2001. [S3]
- Tie to the exam: named in 2.1.3 ("Bloom's Taxonomy"; revision is the version most ID texts now use).
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: ordered, lower to higher complexity; the "requirement of a strict hierarchy has been relaxed" so categories may overlap. [S3]
- Items (verbs, in order, with the paper's subcategories):
  1. Remember: recognizing, recalling. [S3]
  2. Understand: interpreting, exemplifying, classifying, summarizing, inferring, comparing, explaining. [S3]
  3. Apply: executing, implementing. [S3]
  4. Analyze: differentiating, organizing, attributing. [S3]
  5. Evaluate: checking, critiquing. [S3]
  6. Create: generating, planning, producing. [S3]
- Changes from the original [S3]:
  - Category names changed from nouns to verbs; Comprehension became Understand. 
  - "Synthesis changed places with Evaluation and was renamed Create." [S3] So Create is the top level, Evaluate is fifth.
  - The original Knowledge category was split: its noun part became a separate Knowledge dimension; its verb part became Remember.
  - Two dimensions form a Taxonomy Table (Knowledge on the vertical axis, Cognitive Process on the horizontal); each objective sits in a cell.
  - Strict cumulative hierarchy relaxed.
- Confusables: original order (Synthesis below Evaluation) vs revised (Evaluate below Create) is the classic trap. "Knowledge" (original level 1) vs "Remember" (revised level 1) vs "Knowledge dimension."
- Variants or contested points: none in the source; the 2001 work has eight contributors, so "Anderson & Krathwohl" is the usual short attribution.
- Unverified: none for the list itself.

### Bloom's revised taxonomy: Knowledge dimension
- Origin: Anderson & Krathwohl et al., 2001. [S3]
- Tie to the exam: 2.1.3 (part of Bloom's revised taxonomy).
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: ordered, concrete to abstract (A to D, as the paper letters them).
- Items:
  1. Factual Knowledge: terminology; specific details and elements. [S3]
  2. Conceptual Knowledge: classifications and categories; principles and generalizations; theories, models and structures. [S3]
  3. Procedural Knowledge: subject-specific skills and algorithms; techniques and methods; criteria for when to use procedures. [S3]
  4. Metacognitive Knowledge (new in the revision): strategic knowledge; knowledge about cognitive tasks (including contextual and conditional); self-knowledge. [S3]
- Confusables: the cognitive process dimension; Gagné's outcomes (verbal information ≈ factual; intellectual skills; cognitive strategies ≈ metacognitive).
- Variants or contested points: Metacognitive was added in 2001; the 1956 Knowledge category had three subcategories (specifics; ways and means; universals and abstractions). [S3]
- Unverified: none.

### Bloom's three domains of learning (cognitive, affective, psychomotor)
- Origin: the 1956 group planned three domains. Handbook I (cognitive) 1956; Handbook II (affective) by Krathwohl, Bloom & Masia, 1964; a psychomotor taxonomy "was never published by the originating group," but Simpson, Dave and Harrow each published one. [S3]
- Tie to the exam: 2.1.3 (Bloom's Taxonomy); the deck [S4] has a card asking which domain a skill belongs to, so domain-classification questions are plausible.
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: set
- Items:
  1. Cognitive: knowledge and intellectual skills (the 1956 handbook). [S3]
  2. Affective: feelings, values, attitudes (1964 handbook). [S3] for existence and authors
  3. Psychomotor: physical/motor skills (versions by Simpson, Dave, Harrow). [S3] for existence and authors
- Confusables: KSA (knowledge, skills, attitudes) is the common shorthand mapping (cognitive = knowledge, psychomotor = skills, affective = attitudes), not Bloom's own terms. Gagné's five outcomes also include "motor skills" and "attitudes."
- Variants or contested points: there is no single "Bloom" psychomotor taxonomy; any question naming Bloom's psychomotor levels is ambiguous. The deck [S4] places "listening skills" in the affective domain, which is debatable.
- Affective domain levels (gap fill), ordered from lowest to highest internalization, per [S14] citing Krathwohl, Bloom & Masia, Handbook II:
  1. Receiving: awareness of and willingness to attend to a stimulus. [S14]
  2. Responding: active participation (compliance, willingness, satisfaction in responding). [S14]
  3. Valuing: behavior that shows an internalized value and is visible and consistent. [S14]
  4. Organization: ordering values into priorities and relating them to one another. [S14]
  5. Internalizing (characterization): a value system that consistently guides behavior. [S14] "Internalizing (characterization)"
  - Naming note: [S14] says "Internalizing (characterization)"; the 1964 handbook's own phrase, "Characterization by a value or value complex," is general knowledge and was not seen. Authors can accept either "Characterization" or "Internalizing" but shouldn't make a question hinge on which one.
  - Date note: [S14] dates Handbook II to 1956, but [S3], whose author co-wrote the handbook, gives 1964. Use 1964.
- Unverified: psychomotor levels. [S14] cites Simpson (1972), but the fetched text showed six levels starting "Guided Response, Set, Mechanism…", with no Perception, which conflicts with the usual seven-level Simpson order (Perception, Set, Guided Response, Mechanism, Complex Overt Response, Adaptation, Origination). Probably a fetch or layout error, but unresolved. Dave's and Harrow's versions weren't checked. Don't author psychomotor-level questions. The affective levels can be authored with tag `named`; their source tier is "other" (a university page).

### Gagné's Nine Events of Instruction (outline wording: "Gagne's Nine Levels of Learning")
- Origin: Robert M. Gagné, The Conditions of Learning (first ed. 1965) and later Principles of Instructional Design (with Briggs; later Wager, Golas, Keller). Gap fill: [S13] reproduces the events and processes from Gagné, Wager, Golas & Keller, Principles of Instructional Design, 5th ed., 2005. Gagné's own text wasn't fetched, and the 1965 date is still general knowledge.
- Tie to the exam: named in 2.1.3. The handbook outline calls it "Gagne's Nine Levels of Learning," which is not the usual name; the standard name is Nine Events of Instruction. The deck [S4] asks "after gaining attention, what is next?", so sequence questions are likely.
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: ordered (the sequence of events in a lesson)
- Items (as named in [S5]; the names [S13] reproduces from Gagné, Wager, Golas & Keller 2005 are in brackets, then the internal cognitive process [S13] pairs with each event). Gap fill: [S13] confirms the same nine events in the same order.
  1. Gain attention: focus learners with a question, quote, statistic, image or short video. [S5] [S13: "Gaining attention"; process: Reception]
  2. Inform learners of the objective: state what learners will learn, do and value. [S5] [S13: "Informing learner of objectives"; process: Expectancy]
  3. Stimulate recall of prior learning: connect new material to what learners already know. [S5] [S13: "Stimulate recall of prior learning"; process: Retrieval]
  4. Present the content: structured, segmented material in varied formats. [S5] [S13: "Presenting stimulus"; process: Selective perception]
  5. Provide learning guidance: tips, examples, analogies, case studies, visuals, scaffolding. [S5] [S13: "Providing learning guidance"; process: Semantic encoding]
  6. Elicit performance: practice the new skill. [S5] [S13: "Eliciting performance"; process: Responding]
  7. Provide feedback: timely feedback so learners see gaps. [S5] [S13: "Providing feedback"; process: Reinforcement]
  8. Assess performance: learners demonstrate the outcome. [S5] [S13: "Assessing performance"; process: Retrieval]
  9. Enhance retention and transfer: repeated, varied practice and application to real-world contexts. [S5] [S13: "Enhancing retention and transfer"; process: Generalization]
- Groups: [S5] groups events 1–3 as planning and 4–9 as design/development and delivery (that grouping is the handout's own, not Gagné's).
- Event–process pairs (gap fill): pairs such as "informing learners of the objective = expectancy" or "presenting the stimulus = selective perception" can be tested from [S13]. "Retrieval" appears twice (events 3 and 8), so a question asking which event pairs with "Retrieval" has two right answers; avoid it.
- Confusables: Gagné's five learned capabilities (below), which are "types of learning," not events. Gagné's older "eight types of learning" hierarchy (signal learning … problem solving) may be what "Nine Levels of Learning" garbles; do not treat "levels" as Bloom-style levels. Merrill's First Principles and Keller's ARCS overlap with events 1 and 2.
- Variants or contested points: Gagné's own event names differ slightly by edition (for example "Presenting the stimulus" / "stimulus material" for event 4; "Stimulating recall of prerequisite learning"; "Providing feedback about performance correctness"). The exam's own label is "Nine Levels of Learning."
- Variants (gap fill): other summaries word the processes slightly differently (for example "retrieval to working memory" for event 3, "retrieval and reinforcement" for event 8, "retrieval and generalization" for event 9). Use [S13]'s single-word terms.
- Unverified: (a) resolved in the gap fill: the event–process pairs come from [S13], which reproduces Gagné et al. 2005. The explicit link to information-processing theory is still general knowledge, though [S13] calls its column "Cognitive process"; (b) the first-publication year (1965) is from general knowledge; (c) whether the TDBoK uses "events" or "levels" is still unknown (not attempted: the TDBoK isn't public).

### Gagné's five categories of learning outcomes (learned capabilities)
- Origin: Gagné, The Conditions of Learning. Not confirmed from Gagné's text here.
- Tie to the exam: 2.1.3 (part of Gagné's theory); the deck [S4] has a card on it ("learning standard operating procedures is intellectual skills"), which suggests the TDBoK covers it.
- Suggested tag: extra (only deck evidence of TDBoK coverage)
- Kind: set
- Items:
  1. Verbal information: stating facts, names, organized knowledge. [S4]
  2. Intellectual skills: using concepts, rules and procedures (the deck files SOPs here). [S4]
  3. Cognitive strategies: managing one's own learning and thinking. [S4]

### Knowles' Adult Learning Theory (andragogy): core assumptions/principles
- Origin: Malcolm Knowles; andragogy popularized in the 1970s (The Modern Practice of Adult Education, 1970/1980); current form in The Adult Learner: Knowles, Holton & Swanson (1998 ed., to which [S10]'s figure is credited); the 10th ed. (Routledge 2025, on the ATD list [S1]) is by Knowles, Holton, Robinson & Caraccioli.
- Tie to the exam: named in 2.1.3 ("Knowles' Adult Learning Theory"); The Adult Learner is on ATD's 2.1 reading list [S1].
- Suggested tag: reflist
- Kind: set (numbered 1–6 in the order below in [S10], but not a sequence)
- Items. Gap fill: the names and order are now confirmed by [S10], whose Figure 2.3 labels them "Core Adult Learning Principles" and credits Knowles, Holton & Swanson 1998. [S10]'s sub-points are in parentheses; the ATD blog's labels are in brackets:
  1. Learner's Need to Know (why, what, how): adults need to know why they should learn something before learning it. [S10] [S6 as "Need for Knowledge"] "Adults need to know 'why' they should learn."
  2. Self-Concept of the Learner (autonomous, self-directing): adults see themselves as responsible for their own decisions and want to be self-directing. [S10] [S6 as "Self-Direction"]
  3. Prior Experience of the Learner (resource, mental models): adults bring a large, varied store of experience that is a resource for learning (and a source of bias). [S10] [S6 as "Foundation or Experience"] "rich reserves of experiences"
  4. Readiness to Learn (life related, developmental task): adults become ready to learn what they need for real-life situations and roles. [S10] [S6 as "Willingness"]
  5. Orientation to Learning (problem centered, contextual): problem-, task- or life-centered rather than subject-centered. [S10] [S6 as "Orientation to Learning"]
  6. Motivation to Learn (intrinsic value, personal payoff): the strongest motivators are internal (self-esteem, job satisfaction, quality of life). [S10] [S6 as "Motivation"] "Adults are driven by internal motives."
  - [S15] (a university page) gives the same six with slightly different labels ("Experience of the learner"; "Need to know" listed last).
- Confusables: pedagogy (teacher-directed; the deck [S4] contrasts external motivation in pedagogy with internal motivation in andragogy); self-directed learning; Rogers' learner-centered approach ([S4] suggests the TDBoK covers Rogers); Mezirow's transformative learning (also "adult learning theory").
- Variants or contested points: the count varies by date. Knowles' early work had four assumptions (self-concept, experience, readiness, orientation); motivation was added later, and need to know later still, giving six in The Adult Learner. Some sources say "five assumptions"; [S15] contradicts itself, saying both "six core assumptions" and "five assumptions… (a sixth was later added)." The ATD blog [S6] uses its own non-standard labels.
- Which version ATD uses (gap fill): ATD's 2.1 reading list [S1] names The Adult Learner (10th ed., 2025). The six-principle Andragogy in Practice figure in [S10] is credited to the 1998 edition of that book, so six is the version on ATD's list. The 10th edition itself wasn't checked; it is very likely, though unconfirmed, that it still has six. Authors should use six and [S10]'s names.
- Unverified: the four-then-five-then-six history (which works and years added motivation and need to know) is still general knowledge; [S10] doesn't give it. Don't write questions on the history or on "how many assumptions did Knowles originally propose."

### Kolb's Experiential Learning Cycle (experiential learning)
- Origin: David A. Kolb, Experiential Learning: Experience as the Source of Learning and Development, 1984 (builds on Dewey, Lewin, Piaget). [S6] names Kolb; book details are general knowledge.
- Tie to the exam: 2.1.3 names "experiential learning"; Kolb is the standard model for it. Not on the ATD list by name.
- Suggested tag: named (the statement names experiential learning; Kolb is the model for it)
- Kind: ordered cycle, CE → RO → AC → AE (confirmed in the gap fill by [S11]: concrete experiences "are the basis for observations and reflections," which are distilled into abstract concepts and then actively tested). Unverified: the common claim that learners can enter at any point; Kolb & Kolb 2005 [S11] don't say it, so don't use it.
- Kolb's definition of learning (gap fill): "the process whereby knowledge is created through the transformation of experience." [S11]
- Items:
  1. Concrete Experience (CE): having a direct experience. [S6] [S11]
  2. Reflective Observation (RO): reflecting on that experience. [S6] [S11] "engage with and reflect on their experiences"
  3. Abstract Conceptualization (AC): forming concepts or generalizations from the reflections. [S6] [S11]
  4. Active Experimentation (AE): testing ideas through hands-on tasks (role plays, internships). [S6] [S11]
- Groups (gap fill): two dialectical dimensions. CE and AC are the two modes of grasping experience; RO and AE are the two modes of transforming it. [S11] An ideal learner "touches all the bases." [S11]
- Confusables: Kolb's four learning styles (below); Honey & Mumford's styles (Activist, Reflector, Theorist, Pragmatist), which map onto the same cycle; Lewin's experiential model; Kirkpatrick-style "levels"; 70-20-10 (learning from experience on the job).
- Variants or contested points: learning styles as a basis for matching instruction is widely disputed (the "learning styles myth"); the cycle itself is not.
- Unverified: resolved in the gap fill (styles: see the next entry). The 1984 book's details are still general knowledge.

### Mager's Criterion-Referenced Instruction (CRI) approach: stages
- Origin: Robert F. Mager (studies in the early 1960s, per [S7]); usually cited to Mager & Pipe, Criterion Referenced Instruction (book; edition years vary). [S7] says CRI draws on Gagné's Nine Events.
- Tie to the exam: named in 2.1.3 ("Mager's Criterion-Referenced Instruction Approach").
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: ordered (design sequence)
- Items:
  1. Task analysis or goal analysis: identify what needs to be learned (what the job requires). [S7]
  2. Performance objectives: specify the outcomes and how they will be evaluated. [S7]
  3. Criterion-referenced testing: decide how to evaluate whether each objective has been met (against the objective's criterion, not against other learners). [S7]
  4. Development: build learning modules tied to the objectives. [S7]
- Confusables: ADDIE phases; Bloom's levels (objectives classification, not a design sequence); norm-referenced testing (comparison with other learners, the opposite of criterion-referenced).
- Variants or contested points: the stage names vary slightly between summaries ("goal analysis" vs "task analysis"; "development of learning modules").
- Unverified: book title and year for Mager & Pipe's CRI (general knowledge only).

### Bandura's self-efficacy (gap fill)
- Origin: Bandura, "Self-efficacy: Toward a Unifying Theory of Behavioral Change," Psychological Review, 1977. [S12]
- Tie to the exam: 2.1.3 (social learning; part of Bandura's theory). Not named in the statement.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set (the four sources); [S12] says performance accomplishments are the most influential.
- Definitions:
  - Efficacy expectation: "the conviction that one can successfully execute the behavior" required for an outcome. [S12]
  - Outcome expectancy: a person's estimate that a behavior will lead to certain outcomes. [S12] Confusable pair: self-efficacy is about "can I do it?", outcome expectancy about "will it work?"
- Items (the four sources of efficacy expectations, in [S12]'s order):
  1. Performance accomplishments: personal mastery experiences; the most influential source. [S12] "especially influential"
  2. Vicarious experience: seeing others perform successfully (modeling). [S12]

- Variants or contested points: renamed sources in later works, as above (general knowledge).
