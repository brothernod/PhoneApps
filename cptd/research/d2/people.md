## 2.7.2 and 2.7.3: Coaching (goals, action plans, development; coaching managers)

The two 2.7 reading-list books serve both 2.7 statements. Everyday Coaching is explicitly aimed at managers having coaching conversations, so it is the closest fit for 2.7.3; its "Construct a Plan" / "Commit to Action" steps fit 2.7.2.

### Seven Cs (Everyday Coaching model)
- Origin: Virginia Bianco-Mathis and Lisa Nabors, 2017, *Everyday Coaching: Using Conversation to Strengthen Your Culture* (ATD Press). Part 2 of the book is titled "Navigating the Seven Cs". [S3]
- Tie to the exam: on ATD's reading list under 2.7 Coaching. [S1]
- Suggested tag: reflist
- Kind: ordered (the book's chapter sequence, first to last stage of a coaching conversation/engagement)
- Items, in the source's own terms (the seven C-verbs as they appear in the titles of chapters 3–6):
  1. Capture Context: understand the situation, the person and their environment (ch. 3, "Capture Context and Clarify Purpose"). [S3]
  2. Clarify Purpose: agree what the coaching is for (ch. 3). [S3]
  3. Collect (and Feed Back) Data: gather information, such as observations or assessments, and share it with the coachee (ch. 4, "Collect and Feed Back Data"). [S3]
  4. Create Options: generate possible ways forward (ch. 5, "Create Options and Construct a Plan"). [S3]
  5. Construct a Plan: turn chosen options into a plan (ch. 5). [S3]
  6. Commit to Action: secure the coachee's commitment to act (ch. 6, "Commit to Action and Celebrate Success"). [S3]
  7. Celebrate Success: recognize progress and results (ch. 6). [S3]
- Groups: the book pairs them by chapter: ch. 3 (Capture, Clarify), ch. 4 (Collect), ch. 5 (Create, Construct), ch. 6 (Commit, Celebrate). [S3]
- Other parts of the book: Part 1 "Connecting Through Coaching" (ch. 1 Changing the Conversation; ch. 2 Dialogue: Learning the Language of Coaching); Part 3 "Building a Coaching Culture" (ch. 7 Implementing a Systems Infrastructure; ch. 8 Looking to the Future). The publisher describes it as an "easy-to-use coaching model" for feedback, agreements and guiding behavior. [S3]
- Confusables: GROW (Goal, Reality, Options, Will) also has an "options" step; Haneberg's coaching model (also has goals, agreement, action planning); the "Seven Cs of communication" (a different, unrelated list).
- Variants or contested points: the reading list spells the title "Every Day Coaching"; the book is "Everyday Coaching". The O'Reilly page names the seven only through chapter titles; the count of seven matches the seven C-verbs in those titles, but the book's own one-word labels (e.g. "Collect" vs "Collect Data") weren't seen.
- Unverified: the authors' own one-line definitions of each C (paraphrases above are inferred from the chapter titles only). Whether the book presents the Cs as strictly sequential or as a navigable cycle ("Navigating" suggests the latter). Authors should test only the seven names and their order.

### Haneberg's coaching model (Coaching Basics)
- Origin: Lisa Haneberg, *Coaching Basics*, 2nd ed., 2016, ATD Press (Training Basics series), ch. 2 "A Coaching Model". Draws on a survey of about 40 experienced coaches. [S4]
- Tie to the exam: on ATD's reading list under 2.7 Coaching. [S1]
- Suggested tag: reflist
- Kind: ordered (chapter 2's sub-headings, start to end of a coaching engagement)
- Items, in the source's own terms (chapter 2 sub-headings as listed in the O'Reilly table of contents):
  1. Establish a Relationship With the Performer. [S4]
  2. Opportunity and Offer: the coach sees a coaching opportunity and offers coaching. [S4]
  3. Request for Coaching: the performer asks for coaching. [S4]
  4. Assess Interest and Coachability. [S4]
  5. Clarify Goals. [S4]
  6. Gain Agreement. [S4]
  7. Great Coaching Dialogue. [S4]
  8. Action Planning. [S4]
  9. Helping Performers Take Action. [S4]
  10. Conclude Coaching. [S4]
- Other named concepts in the book: "Coachability" (ch. 5), "Building Performer Self-Awareness" (ch. 6), "Helping Performers Get Unstuck" (ch. 7), "Breakthrough Catalysts" and "Breakthrough Inhibitors" (ch. 8, Facilitating Breakthroughs), "Microcoaching" (ch. 9), "Coaching by Walking Around" (ch. 3). Haneberg calls the person coached the "performer". [S4]
- Confusables: Seven Cs (Everyday Coaching); GROW; ICF core competencies (also has "agreements" and "facilitates growth").
- Variants or contested points: items 2 and 3 look like two alternative ways coaching starts (coach offers vs performer requests), not two sequential steps. Don't build an ordering question that depends on 2 vs 3.
- Unverified: whether all ten headings are steps of the model or some are supporting sections; their exact descriptions. Treat this model as low-confidence for ordering; safest uses are "which book/author uses the term performer, coachability, microcoaching".

### SBI feedback (Situation-Behavior-Impact), as a manager-coaching tool
- Origin: Center for Creative Leadership (CCL). The UMN HR page describing "everyday coaching" for supervisors uses SBI but doesn't credit it. [S5]
- Tie to the exam: general knowledge for 2.7.3 (a method managers use to support development). Not seen on the reading list or in a TDBoK excerpt.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: ordered (describe situation, then behavior, then impact)
- Items: 1. Situation: when and where it happened. 2. Behavior: the observable action. 3. Impact: the effect it had. Then ask what the employee will do differently or keep doing. [S5]
- Confusables: Domain 1 feedback models (the app may already have SBI in Domain 1; check `kin`/duplicates before adding).
- Unverified: CCL attribution was not fetched this session (general knowledge). Check against Domain 1's source file if SBI already exists there.

### Manager practices for everyday development coaching (UMN HR)
- Origin: University of Minnesota Office of Human Resources, "Everyday Coaching" (supervisor guidance). Uses David Peterson's definition of coaching: "equipping people with the tools, knowledge, and opportunities" to develop. [S5]
- Tie to the exam: general knowledge for 2.7.3. Not ATD.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set
- Items: feedback builds awareness of what happened, coaching helps the employee decide what to do next; pause for reflection and connect learning back to work (after projects, courses); create opportunities to practice (stretch assignments, new responsibilities, special projects); make development part of one-on-ones; "2+2" (two observations on progress, two actionable suggestions). [S5]
- Unverified: not tier 1–3 for the exam. Use only as background for case scenarios, not as a named model.

### GROW model
- Origin: Sir John Whitmore "and his peers", late 1980s; popularized in Whitmore's *Coaching for Performance* (Performance Consultants). [S6]
- Tie to the exam: general knowledge for 2.7.2 (goal identification, options, action/commitment). Not on the 2025 reading list and not seen in a TDBoK excerpt this session. It is the most widely taught coaching model, so expect it as background or as a distractor.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: ordered (conventional sequence, first to last)
- Items, in the source's own terms (the owner's page lists them as "Goals", "Reality", "Options" and "Will"; "Goal" singular is the common form):
  1. Goal(s): what the coachee wants to achieve. [S6]
  2. Reality: the current situation. [S6]
  3. Options: possible courses of action. [S6]
  4. Will: what the coachee will do, and their commitment. [S6] The owner's page says W stands for "Will".
- Confusables: Seven Cs (Create Options); Haneberg's model (Clarify Goals, Action Planning); SMART goals (used inside the G stage); "Way forward" or "What/When/Whom" versions of W.
- Variants or contested points: W is often given as "Way Forward" (common in UK and NHS materials) or "What will you do"; the owner's page uses Will. [S6] Origin is often credited jointly to Graham Alexander, Alan Fine and Whitmore; the owner's page credits only "Sir John Whitmore and his peers". [S6] The owner's page says GROW is used "in conjunction with" core coaching skills, not "in isolation", and frames coaching as "collaborative" rather than "command and control". [S6]
- Unverified: Whitmore's book's guidance that sessions may start with Reality when the goal is unclear, and his "awareness and responsibility" principle (general knowledge; not on the fetched page). Stage definitions above are general paraphrase; the owner's page doesn't define them.

## 2.6.6: Qualification programs

No ATD reading-list title covers this, and the TDBoK chapter wasn't available. The only ATD material found treats "qualification" through credentials: certificate programs vs certification. Authors should keep questions to the distinctions below.

### Certificate program vs certification
- Origin: ATD blog post "Distinctions Between Certification and Certificate Programs", Feb 21, 2013, drawing on a November 2010 *T+D* article and an Infoline by Sara Bushman (February 2013). No blog author named. [S7]
- Tie to the exam: ATD material on how to develop credential programs; the closest ATD tie to 2.6.6 found. Not on the reading list; TDBoK link not shown.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set (two contrasting credential types)
- Items, in the source's own terms:
  1. Certificate program: "used to develop an individual's knowledge, skills, and competencies"; includes a learning event (training) and an assessment in which participants "demonstrate mastery in a specific content area". [S7]
  2. Certification: "used primarily to test existing knowledge, skills, and competencies"; "does not include a learning event or training of any kind". [S7]
- Confusables: certificate of attendance/participation (no assessment); licensure (government-mandated to practice); accreditation (applies to programs or organizations, not individuals); digital badges. None of these are in the fetched ATD text.
- Variants or contested points: the original comparison table (who awards, expiry, recertification, role analysis) was an image and could not be read. [S7]
- Unverified: everything about who awards each, recertification, or job-analysis basis. Don't test those from this source.

### Steps for building a certificate program (ATD Infoline outline)
- Origin: Sara Bushman, ATD Infoline (February 2013), as previewed on the ATD blog. [S7]
- Tie to the exam: ATD's process for developing and implementing a credential-style qualification program (2.6.6 "develop and implement").
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: ordered (the Infoline's sequence of topics, first to last; it's a table of contents, not a formally named model)
- Items, in the source's own terms (paraphrased topic headings):
  1. Differentiate between certificate programs and certification. [S7]
  2. Determine needs and capabilities for building a certificate program. [S7]
  3. Design, develop and implement the certificate program step by step. [S7]
  4. Create a program evaluation. [S7]
  5. Plan for program maintenance. [S7]
- Also mentioned: a job aid to evaluate the program, a sample timeline and a checklist (members only). [S7]
- Confusables: ADDIE (analyze, design, develop, implement, evaluate) maps almost one-to-one onto items 2–4; authors should not write a question where ADDIE and this list are both defensible.
- Unverified: the detailed steps inside item 3 (members-only). Low-confidence as a "model"; better used for a case step than an ordering card.

### Credential types: assessment-based certificate vs certification vs certificate of attendance (ICE)
- Origin: Institute for Credentialing Excellence (ICE), "Certificate vs Certification" (accreditation guidance). ICE accredits certification programs through NCCA and certificate programs through ACAP against the ICE 1100 standard. [S8]
- Tie to the exam: general knowledge for 2.6.6 (designing a qualification program means choosing which kind of credential it is). Corroborates and extends the ATD blog [S7]. Not on the reading list; no TDBoK link shown.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set (three credential types)
- Items, in the source's own terms:
  1. Assessment-based certificate: provides instruction and training (non-degree); the assessment is "linked directly to the learning event" and "may be narrower in scope"; awards a certificate for mastery of specific learning outcomes; accredited under the "ICE 1100 Standard". [S8]
  2. Certification: "Assesses knowledge, skills, and/or competencies previously acquired"; assessment is "independent of a specific learning event" and "usually broad in scope"; "Awards designations"; accredited under the "NCCA Standards for the Accreditation of Certification Programs". [S8]
  3. Certificate of attendance or participation: given for having "attended or participated in a course or training program"; "NOT a certificate of mastery". [S8]
- Confusables: ATD's two-way certificate vs certification split [S7] (same idea, fewer types). Licensure and accreditation (not on this page). CPTD itself is a certification (tests existing competence, awards a designation), which makes a good worked example.
- Variants or contested points: ICE's full guidance document lists 12 distinctions; not fetched. [S8]
- Unverified: licensure (government permission to practice), ISO/IEC 17024, recertification and job/practice analysis (role delineation) as the basis of certification exams. These are general knowledge, not on the fetched page.

### Strengths-based leadership development (Zenger and Folkman findings)
- Origin: Zenger and Folkman, "Key Insights from the Extraordinary Leader", 2019. [S9]
- Tie to the exam: on ATD's reading list under 2.6 (same book); bears on designing leadership development experiences. [S1]
- Suggested tag: reflist
- Kind: set (findings, not an ordered model)
- Items, in the source's own terms:
  1. Strengths, not absence of weakness: great leaders are defined by "the presence of clear strengths". [S9]
  2. Number of strengths: one strength put leaders at about the 60th percentile, three at the 80th; "Five strengths catapulted them into the 90th percentile." [S9]
  3. Fatal flaws: "FATAL FLAWS MUST BE FIXED"; examples are character flaws, not learning from mistakes, poor interpersonal skills, rejecting new ideas, lack of accountability, lack of initiative. [S9]
  4. Combinations/cross-training: strengths in different clusters multiply; strong Interpersonal Skills plus Focus on Results raised the chance of being seen as a great leader to 66 percent. [S9]
  5. Competency companions: behaviors that rise and fall with a competency (e.g. "communicating clear expectations" tracks with inspiring and motivating others); work on the companion to move a hard competency. [S9]
  6. Working only on weaknesses produced "only a third of the progress" of working on strengths. [S9]
  7. Organizational ceiling: leaders' scores "rarely exceeded the scores of the most senior leader." [S9]
  8. "We attest that leaders are made." [S9]
- Confusables: Gallup/Clifton strengths (different, not ATD-listed); the 70-20-10 guideline (a different "how leaders develop" claim).
- Variants or contested points: percentile figures differ between Zenger Folkman publications (some say three strengths reach about the 80th, some five reach the 90th). Use the excerpt's numbers and don't make the exact percentile the only thing a question hinges on.
- Unverified: none beyond the variant note.

### Seven dimensions of career growth (Promotions Are So Yesterday)
- Origin: Julie Winkle Giulioni, *Promotions Are So Yesterday: Redefine Career Development. Help Employees Thrive.* (ATD, March 2022). ATD's product page calls it an "easy-to-apply framework of seven alternative dimensions of development". [S10]
- Tie to the exam: on ATD's reading list under 2.6 Career & Leadership Development. [S1] Its content (managers growing people without promotions) also fits 2.7.3 (coaching managers on supporting employee development) and 2.7.2 ("seek development opportunities").
- Suggested tag: reflist
- Kind: set (alternatives to upward movement; no order given)
- Items, in the source's own terms (names from ATD's product page; it gives no definitions, so the one-liners are general paraphrase and unverified):
  1. Contribution: growing the value or impact one adds. [S10]
  2. Competence: building skills and knowledge. [S10]
  3. Confidence: growing self-assurance in one's abilities. [S10]
  4. Connection: building relationships and networks. [S10]
  5. Challenge: taking on stretch and novelty. [S10]
  6. Contentment: growing satisfaction or meaning in the work. [S10]
  7. Choice: gaining options and control over one's work and path. [S10]
- Other points on the page: "managers are in a powerful position to redefine career development"; these growth opportunities are "completely within your control as a manager"; the book offers templates and "Nearly 100 questions" for career dialogue. [S10]
- Confusables: Seven Cs of Everyday Coaching (also seven C-words; also an ATD 2.7-adjacent book). This is a real risk for "which model?" cards: Seven Cs items are verbs (Capture, Clarify, Collect, Create, Construct, Commit, Celebrate), these are nouns. Put a note in both models; consider not using a "which" card for either. Also: lattice vs ladder careers; Kaye and Winkle Giulioni's earlier *Help Them Grow or Watch Them Go* (not on the 2025 list).
- Variants or contested points: none found for the names.
- Unverified: the author's own definition of each dimension (the paraphrases above are not from the source). Authors should test the names and the "grow without promotion" idea, not the definitions.

### 70-20-10 rule (how leaders learn)
- Origin: Center for Creative Leadership (CCL); the framework "emerged from over 30 years of our *Lessons of Experience* research" on how executives learn over their careers. The page credits Cindy McCauley ("Based on Research by"). [S11]
- Tie to the exam: general knowledge for 2.6.8 (designing leadership development experiences as a mix of assignments, relationships and courses). Not on the 2025 reading list; TDBoK link not shown this session. Widely used in ATD material (also likely in the `learn` or `design` shard: check for a duplicate model before authoring, and add `kin` if both exist).
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set (three sources with proportions; usually listed largest to smallest)
- Items, in the source's own terms:
  1. 70%: "challenging experiences and assignments". [S11]
  2. 20%: "developmental relationships". [S11]
  3. 10%: "coursework and training". [S11]
- Confusables: Bersin/Jennings' informal vs formal learning framing; ATD's "3-33" and other alternatives (not covered here); CCL's five sources of leadership learning (below), which are kinds of the 70%.
- Variants or contested points: CCL calls it "the 70-20-10 rule", a "Framework" and a "classic guideline for developing managers". [S11] It doesn't say the numbers are exact (it says individuals "tend to learn" in these shares). [S11] Common misattribution: often credited to Lombardo and Eichinger (*The Career Architect Development Planner*, 1996) or to Morgan McCall; the fetched CCL page names neither. Many sources say the percentages are not empirical prescriptions; don't test that claim from this file.
- Unverified: the Lombardo and Eichinger / McCall origin, any year or sample size (general knowledge; not on the fetched page).
