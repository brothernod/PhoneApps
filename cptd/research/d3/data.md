### Qualitative vs quantitative data
- Origin: [S4] index entries "qualitative data", "quantitative data" and "categorical data" (definitions on pages outside the sample); [S5] ch. 8, "When the words have the meaning: visualizing qualitative data"; [S7] card 51.

  2. Qualitative data: non-numeric data such as words, images or observations (open-ended comments, interview notes, focus group transcripts), analyzed for themes. The Lyons and Evergreen chooser names "Thematic Analysis" as a route [S11].

- Unverified: TDBoK and Torrance definitions not seen.

### Levels (scales) of measurement: Stevens's four scales
- Origin: S. S. Stevens, "On the Theory of Scales of Measurement", *Science*, 1946 [S13].
- Tie to the exam: 3.7.2 and 3.7.4 (which statistics suit which data); named in the shard's ownership brief. Torrance's index has "categorical data" [S4], but no ATD source listing the four scales was seen.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: ordered (lowest to highest level; each scale has the properties of the ones before it, and its permissible statistics are cumulative)
- Items, Stevens's terms [S13]:
  1. Nominal: categories or labels with no order; operation "Determination of equality". Permissible statistics: number of cases, mode, contingency correlation. Stevens's examples: numbering football players, numbering classes. L&D examples (general): department, job role, course name, yes/no.
  2. Ordinal: ranked categories, intervals not equal; operation "Determination of greater or less". Statistics: median, percentiles. Examples: mineral hardness, grades of leather; Stevens notes most psychological scales are ordinal. L&D (general): rankings, proficiency levels, Likert ratings strictly speaking.
  3. Interval: equal intervals but no true zero; operation "Determination of equality of intervals or differences". Statistics: mean, standard deviation, rank-order and product-moment correlation. Examples: Celsius and Fahrenheit temperature, calendar dates.
  4. Ratio: equal intervals and a true zero, so ratios are meaningful; operation "Determination of equality of ratios". Statistics: coefficient of variation, plus all the above. Examples: length, weight, counts. L&D (general): hours of training, cost, number of errors, sales.
- Groups (general usage, not Stevens): categorical / qualitative = nominal, ordinal; numerical / quantitative / continuous = interval, ratio.
- Confusables: measures of central tendency (mode, median and mean line up with nominal, ordinal and interval); qualitative vs quantitative; discrete vs continuous.
- Variants or contested points: whether Likert-scale data may be treated as interval (and averaged) is a long-running dispute; common practice averages them. Don't write a question that hinges on it. Stevens's "permissible statistics" rules are themselves debated (Velleman and Wilkinson 1993 appeared in search results; not fetched).
- Unverified: whether the TDBoK covers the four scales at all.

### Structured vs unstructured data
- Origin: general knowledge; not seen in an ATD source.
- Tie to the exam: 3.7.2 ("organizing ... to support retrieval and manipulation"); 3.7.6 (word clouds are a way to visualize unstructured text).
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set
- Items:
  1. Structured: organized in a fixed schema of rows and columns (databases, spreadsheets, LMS completion records, HRIS fields); easy to query and sort.
  2. Semi-structured: tagged but not tabular (JSON, XML, xAPI statements, email headers).
  3. Unstructured: no predefined model (free text, open-ended survey comments, video, chat logs, documents); needs coding or text analytics before analysis.
- Confusables: qualitative vs quantitative (unstructured data is usually qualitative, but not always); big data ([S6] defines it as data "too big or complex for traditional data software").
- Unverified: whether ATD uses a two-way or three-way split. Gap fill 2026-11-09: same failed attempt as primary vs secondary (ODU library guide found, not read). Keep questions to the two-way split (structured vs unstructured); don't key "semi-structured".

### Organizing and cleaning data for retrieval and manipulation
- Origin: general data-management practice; [S4] chapter titles 10 "Build in Data Capture", 11 "Store the Data" and 12 "Iterate on the Data and the Analysis" and the index entry "noisy data layer" show Torrance covers this ground (text not seen); [S7] card 36/50 (analysis in software: pivot tables, plotting, correlations); Wickham's "tidy data" (2014, *Journal of Statistical Software*) is general knowledge, not fetched.
- Tie to the exam: 3.7.2's "logical and/or practical ways to support retrieval and manipulation".
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set (the cleaning steps are often done in roughly this order, but no source order is established)
- Items (general knowledge):
  1. Plan capture up front: decide what to collect and build the data capture into the solution (Torrance ch. 10 title).
  2. Use a consistent structure: one row per record (observation), one column per variable, one value per cell (tidy data); a data dictionary (codebook) defining each field.
  3. Use a common unique identifier (for example employee ID) so sources such as the LMS and HRIS can be joined.
  4. Store securely and centrally with clear naming and version control (Torrance ch. 11 title); restrict access to personal data.
  5. Clean: remove duplicates; fix structural errors (inconsistent spellings, codes, date formats, units); handle missing values (find out why, then exclude, flag or impute); check outliers (error or real?); validate against the source.
  6. Document every cleaning decision so the analysis can be repeated.
- Confusables: data governance and privacy law (Domain 1 GDPR model); `elearn-standards` (xAPI structures learning data at capture: actor, verb, object).
- Unverified: the TDBoK's own steps, if any; Torrance's chapters 10–12 text.

### Five-step data analysis process (define questions → interpret results)
- Origin: deck keyed to TDBoK 3.7 [S7] (cards 34–37, 50, 56–57). The same five steps circulate widely as a generic "data analysis process" (general knowledge; original author not traced this session).
- Tie to the exam: 3.7.3 (step 1 is framing the questions with stakeholders) and 3.7.4 (steps 4–5). TDBoK link shown only by the deck.
- Suggested tag: extra (deck-only TDBoK evidence; per LESSONS.md a deck alone can't carry `tdbok`)
- Kind: ordered (first to last)
- Items [S7, paraphrased cards]:
  1. Define the questions: decide what question(s) the analysis must answer, starting from the stakeholder's goal.
  2. Set clear measurement priorities: decide what to measure and how to measure it.
  3. Collect data: gather relevant data, from existing sources first.
  4. Analyze data: work with the data in software (pivot tables, plotting, finding correlations).
  5. Interpret results: check whether the results answer the original question, and what they mean.
- Confusables: Torrance's book outline [S4] (plan → hypothesis and data needs → sources → capture → store → iterate → communicate and visualize → scale), which is a design-for-data sequence, not this list; ADDIE (`addie`); Kirkpatrick/Phillips evaluation planning (`kirk-levels`, `phillips-levels`); Block's consulting phases (`biz`); action research (`od`).
- Variants or contested points: other generic versions have six steps (ask, prepare, process, analyze, share, act, as in Google's data analytics course) or add "clean the data" as its own step; don't test a step count other than this deck's five, and don't call it "the TDBoK's five steps" with certainty.

## 3.7.4: Analyzing and interpreting results

### Patterns, trends and relationships among variables (the statement's three targets)
- Origin: statement wording [S1], repeated in [S6]. Definitions are general knowledge.
- Tie to the exam: named in 3.7.4.
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: set
- Items:
  1. Patterns: regularities or recurring structures in the data, such as clusters, segments that behave alike, seasonality or outliers that break the pattern.
  2. Trends: the direction of change over time (rising, falling, flat). Evergreen's ch. 9 "How things changed over time: depicting trends" [S5]; line charts are the usual display.

- Confusables: the four analytics types (below); 3.7.3's five framing elements.

### Four types of analytics (descriptive, diagnostic, predictive, prescriptive)
- Origin: Gartner (popularized around 2012–2013 as the "analytic ascendancy model"; reported by Elliott from the 2013 Gartner BI summit [S9]). ATD sources: the TDBoK deck [S7] uses diagnostic and predictive with HR examples; [S10] (CTDO, 2017) describes "descriptive to predictive to prescriptive"; Torrance's index has analytics "categories of" (p. 77) and "levels of" (pp. 22–23) [S4], text not seen.
- Tie to the exam: 3.7.4 (interpreting analyses); named in the shard's ownership brief.
- Suggested tag: extra (deck plus non-ATD report; a TDBoK link is likely but only deck-shown)
- Kind: ordered (lowest to highest value and difficulty, per Gartner [S9] "More Difficult, But More Value")

### Measures of central tendency (mean, median, mode)
- Origin: general statistics; [S4] index lists mean, median and mode in ch. 5 "A Little Bit of Statistics"; [S5] ch. 2 "showing mean, frequency, and measures of variability"; [S7] card 24.
- Tie to the exam: 3.7.4; named in the ownership brief.
- Suggested tag: reflist (covered in Torrance ch. 5 per its index; definitions are standard)
- Kind: set
- Items (standard definitions):
  1. Mean: the arithmetic average (sum ÷ count). Pulled by outliers and skew. Needs interval or ratio data [S13].
  2. Median: the middle value when sorted (half above, half below). Resists outliers; preferred for skewed data such as salaries or time-to-fill. Needs at least ordinal data [S13].
  3. Mode: the most frequent value. The only one usable with nominal data [S13]; a distribution can have more than one mode.
- Pitfall (TDBoK deck): over-relying on the mean while neglecting the median and mode [S7].
- Confusables: measures of dispersion (below); Stevens's scales.
- Unverified: TDBoK wording.

### Measures of dispersion (variability)
- Origin: general statistics; [S4] index lists variance (ch. 5); [S5] ch. 2 "measures of variability".
- Tie to the exam: 3.7.4; named in the ownership brief.
- Suggested tag: reflist (Torrance and Evergreen cover variability; definitions are standard)
- Kind: set
- Items (standard definitions):
  1. Range: highest value minus lowest. Simple; driven entirely by the two extremes.
  2. Interquartile range (IQR): spread of the middle 50% (third quartile minus first). Resists outliers; shown by a box plot.
  3. Variance: the average squared distance from the mean.
  4. Standard deviation: the square root of the variance, in the data's own units. In a normal distribution about 68% of values fall within 1 SD of the mean and about 95% within 2 SD.
- Confusables: central tendency; standard error (the precision of a sample mean, not the spread of the data).
- Unverified: which measures the TDBoK names (deck [S7] doesn't cover dispersion). Gap fill 2026-11-09: no ATD or standard reference fetched (budget and fetch limit). Evergreen's ch. 2 title confirms "measures of variability" is reading-list content [S5] [S16].

### Correlation vs causation
- Origin: general statistics; [S4] index lists correlation and causation (ch. 5) and its references include Vigen, *Spurious Correlations* (2015); [S5] ch. 7 (correlation and regression); [S7] cards 10, 21.
- Tie to the exam: 3.7.4 ("relationships among variables"); named in the ownership brief.
- Suggested tag: reflist
- Kind: set
- Items (standard):
  1. Correlation: a measure of how two variables move together. The correlation coefficient (Pearson's r) runs from −1 (perfect negative) through 0 (none) to +1 (perfect positive); the sign gives the direction, the size gives the strength. Shown with a scatter plot [S7].
  2. Causation: one variable produces the change in the other. Correlation alone does not show it.
  3. Why a correlation may not be causal: a third (confounding) variable drives both; reverse causation (the effect drives the "cause"); coincidence (spurious correlation, Vigen's book [S4]); selection bias.
  4. What supports a causal claim: a control or comparison group, or a randomized experiment; the cause coming before the effect; ruling out other explanations. In evaluation these are the isolation methods in `roi-isolate`.
- Confusables: regression (predicts one variable from others; still not proof of cause); `roi-isolate`.
- Unverified: TDBoK wording.

### Statistical significance and sample size
- Origin: general statistics; not seen in an ATD source this session (not in the Torrance sample or the deck). [S7] card 25 says to establish the sample size (respondents vs the ideal total) before interpreting.
- Tie to the exam: 3.7.4; named in the ownership brief.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set
- Items (standard):
  1. Null hypothesis: the assumption of no effect or no difference.
  2. p-value: the probability of getting a result at least this extreme if the null hypothesis were true.
  3. Significance level (alpha): the cut-off chosen in advance, conventionally 0.05; p below alpha is called statistically significant.
  4. Sample size and response rate: small samples give unstable results and wide margins of error; check how many responded out of how many could have [S7].
  5. Practical vs statistical significance: a statistically significant difference can be too small to matter to the business (effect size).
- Confusables: confidence level (for example 95%) and margin of error, used in survey sampling.
- Unverified: whether the TDBoK covers significance at all.

### The five named visualization techniques
- Origin: statement wording [S1]; uses from the TDBoK deck [S7] and Evergreen's choosers [S11] [S12]; placement in Evergreen's reading-list book from its contents [S16] (gap fill); the rest general knowledge as marked.
- Tie to the exam: every item is named in 3.7.6.
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: set (the order printed in the statement: flow charts, graphs, plots, word clouds, heat maps)
- Items:
  1. Flow charts: diagrams of the steps and decision points in a process, in sequence. Deck: process flow diagrams show the order of tasks [S7]. Lyons and Evergreen's qualitative chooser lists "Flow/Path Diagram" (and a "Journey Map") [S11]. Standard symbols (general knowledge): ovals for start and end, rectangles for steps, diamonds for decisions, arrows for flow. Use for processes, workflows, decision paths, learner journeys; not for quantities.
  2. Graphs: charts that show quantitative values with bars, lines or areas so values can be compared (bar graphs, line graphs). Deck: bar graphs make comparisons visual and suit counts by category (escalations per year); graphs make large data sets coherent [S7].
  3. Plots: charts that place individual data points on axes (scatter plots, dot plots, box plots). Deck: scatter plots show the correlation between two variables (training and tenure) [S7]. The quantitative chooser lists "dot" and "lollipop" charts [S12].
  4. Word clouds: displays of words from text in which each word's size shows how often it appears. For a quick look at open-ended comments (survey free text, discussion posts). Listed on the qualitative chooser [S11]. Limits (general knowledge): show frequency only, lose context and sentiment, split synonyms, need common words removed; not for precise comparison.
  5. Heat maps: a grid (matrix) or map in which color intensity shows the size of each value. Deck: color shows connections among variables affecting performance; values by location, such as sales by outlet [S7]. On both Evergreen choosers ("Heatmap" [S11], "heat map" [S12]). Uses: survey results by item and department, skills-by-role matrices, ratings over time, geography.
- Reading-list corroboration (gap fill, [S16] contents only): "Word Clouds" and "Heat Map" are both subheadings of Evergreen's ch. 8 "When the words have the meaning: visualizing qualitative data". Plots appear as "Scatterplot" and "Connected Scatterplot" (ch. 7, correlation) and "Dot Plot" / "Dumbbell Dot Plot" (ch. 3, comparisons). Graphs appear as "Bar Graph", "Column Graph", "Area Graph" and "Line Graph" (the line graph opens ch. 9, trends). So the definitions of flow charts stay deck plus general knowledge.
- Confusables: graphs vs plots overlap in everyday use ("line plot" vs "line graph"); don't write a question that turns on the difference. A histogram (distribution) vs a bar graph (categories). A heat map (color = value in a grid) vs a choropleth map (color = value by region; a geographic heat map in looser use).
- Unverified: TDBoK definitions of each term; Evergreen's text on each (only her contents were seen).
