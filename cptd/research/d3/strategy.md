## 3.4.16: Performance management strategy

What the reading-list source covers [S6]: the sample names no cycle, rating scale or rating-error list. Its section headings are The Importance of Feedback; Challenges to Great Performance Feedback; Start With Why; How to Provide Feedback Well; What Do You Evaluate?; Systems Approach: Group Behavior; Conclusion; plus a job aid, "Feedback Conversation Preparation Template". [S6] Usable points from it:
- Feedback should be **contemporaneous**: "contemporaneous with the observed behavior" (close in time to what was seen). [S6]
- People improve by **course correcting** after feedback, which the issue ties to classical and operant conditioning. [S6]
- **360-degree assessments** gather input "from a manager, peers" and others. [S6]
- It quotes OPM that feedback "is a critical component of a successful performance management program." [S6]
- It takes a **holistic systems approach**: the organization as interdependent parts (heading "Systems Approach: Group Behavior"). [S6]
- It cites Stone and Heen, *Thanks for the Feedback*. [S6]
- Confusables: Domain 1/2 feedback models already in the app (`sbi`, `grow`); the TDBoK's "Immediate, Sincere, Specific" feedback wording (Domain 1, per LESSONS.md) vs this issue's "contemporaneous". Don't merge them.

### Performance management cycle (OPM five phases)
- Origin: U.S. Office of Personnel Management, federal performance management guidance. [S5]
- Tie to the exam: 3.4.16 (performance management strategy), via general knowledge only. ATD's own reading-list issue quotes OPM on performance management [S6] but its visible part shows no cycle. The TDBoK's own cycle wording was not seen.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: ordered (a repeating cycle; OPM lists the phases in this order and calls it "continuous" [S5])
- Items, in the source's own terms:
  1. Planning: plan work in advance and set expectations and goals. [S5] "Plan work in advance so that expectations and goals can be set"
  2. Monitoring: watch progress and performance all the time, not only at review time. [S5] "Monitor progress and performance continually"
  3. Developing: build the employee's capability through training and work assignments. [S5] "through training and work assignments"
  4. Rating: periodically summarize performance. [S5] "Rate periodically to summarize performance"
  5. Rewarding: recognize good performance. [S5] "Reward good performance"
- Confusables: the many vendor four-stage versions (for example plan, monitor/coach, review, reward), which drop or merge Developing; ADDIE (`addie`) and the PM phases (`pm-phases`), which are also step lists; Kirkpatrick/Phillips evaluation levels; the HPI model and gap analysis (owned by `perf`), which diagnose performance problems rather than run the yearly cycle.
- Variants or contested points: stage names and counts differ widely between HR vendors (three to six stages). OPM's five verbs (Plan, Monitor, Develop, Rate, Reward) are the most widely cited primary version. Which version, if any, the TDBoK uses is unknown.
- Unverified: the TDBoK's treatment of the cycle; whether the exam uses OPM's five names.

### OKRs (Objectives and Key Results)
- Origin: Andy Grove at Intel (1970s), who taught it to John Doerr; Doerr introduced it to Google's founders in 1999 and popularized it in *Measure What Matters* (2018). [S8] "created by Andy Grove at Intel and taught to John Doerr" (Grove's 1970s date and the book's year are general knowledge.)
- Tie to the exam: 3.4.16 (goal setting inside a performance management strategy) and 3.4.4 (setting goals and objectives in a strategic plan). Not named in the statements, the reading list or the ATD glossary [S7]. General knowledge only.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set (two parts), plus a set of five benefits (F.A.C.T.S.)
- Items, in the source's own terms:
  1. Objective: what is to be achieved. [S8] "what is to be achieved, no more and no less"; objectives are "significant, concrete, action oriented". [S8]
  2. Key Results: measurable checkpoints toward the objective. [S8] "benchmark and monitor how we get to the Objective"; they are "measurable and verifiable". [S8] Recommended number: "3-5 supporting Key Results" per objective. [S8]
- Statement form: "I will (Objective) as measured by (Key Results)." [S8] (Usually attributed to Doerr; the page does not attribute it.)
- Benefits, acronym **F.A.C.T.S.** [S8]: Focus; Alignment; Commitment; Tracking; Stretching. (Doerr's book calls these OKR "superpowers"; the fetched page uses the word only in passing. General knowledge.)
- Two types [S8]: **committed** OKRs, "expected to have a passing grade"; **aspirational** OKRs, "sometimes called stretch goals, or 'moonshots.'"
- Scoring: Google scores each key result on "a percentage scale (0.0 - 1.0)" and averages them for the objective. [S8] The often-quoted "0.7 is a good score for stretch goals" was **not** on the page.
- Confusables: MBO (below; OKRs grew out of it [S8]); SMART goals (`smart`, already in the app); KPIs (ongoing health metrics, not time-boxed goals); the balanced scorecard (a measurement framework, not a goal-setting method).
- Variants or contested points: OKRs are usually set quarterly, are often public across the company, are partly bottom-up, and are kept separate from compensation (general knowledge, not on the fetched page). The 0.7 sweet spot is Google lore, unconfirmed here.
- Unverified: quarterly cadence; separation from pay; 0.7 target.

### MBO (Management by Objectives)
- Origin: Peter Drucker, *The Practice of Management* (1954). [S8] says MBOs were "the brainchild of Peter Drucker" and the basis Grove built OKRs on. (The 1954 book is general knowledge.)
- Tie to the exam: 3.4.16 (goal-based appraisal inside performance management). Not named in the statements, the reading list, or the ATD glossary [S7]. General knowledge only.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: ordered (a cycle), as commonly given
- Items (general knowledge; no tier 1–3 step list was fetched):
  1. Set (or review) organizational objectives.
  2. Manager and employee jointly agree on the employee's objectives, cascaded from the organization's.
  3. Monitor progress during the period.
  4. Evaluate (appraise) performance against the agreed objectives.
  5. Give feedback and reward, then set the next period's objectives.
- Key ideas: objectives are **set jointly** (participative) by manager and employee, and appraisal is **against the agreed objectives**, not against traits. Drucker paired it with "self-control" ("management by objectives and self-control", general knowledge).
- Confusables: OKRs (MBO's descendant: OKRs are typically more frequent, more public, include stretch goals and are decoupled from pay, while MBO goals are typically annual, private and tied to compensation; contrast is general knowledge); SMART (`smart`); BARS and other appraisal methods.
- Unverified: every step above; the "self-control" phrase; the OKR/MBO contrast.

### Nine-box grid (9-box talent review)
- Tie to the exam: 3.4.3 (talent reviews inside a talent strategy) and APTD 3.4.12 (identifying high-potential talent) [S2]. Not in the ATD glossary [S7], the statements, or the reading list. General knowledge only.

  2. **Potential** (future capacity to grow into bigger roles): usually the vertical (y) axis.

### SWOT analysis
- Origin: contested. Usually credited to Albert Humphrey and colleagues at the Stanford Research Institute (1960s), or traced to Harvard Business School's business-policy group (Learned, Christensen, Andrews and Guth, *Business Policy: Text and Cases*, 1965). General knowledge; no primary source fetched.
- Tie to the exam: 3.4.4 (assessing the current state while building a strategic plan) and 3.4.5 (weaknesses and threats are anticipated constraints). Not in the statements, the reading list, or the ATD glossary [S7]. General knowledge only.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set (a 2 × 2 grid), with groups
- Items (general knowledge):
  1. Strengths: internal, helpful.
  2. Weaknesses: internal, harmful.
  3. Opportunities: external, helpful.
  4. Threats: external, harmful.
- Groups: **internal** factors (Strengths, Weaknesses; within the organization's control) vs **external** factors (Opportunities, Threats; in the environment). Also **helpful** (S, O) vs **harmful** (W, T).
- Variants: **TOWS matrix** (Heinz Weihrich, 1982, general knowledge) pairs the quadrants into strategies: SO (use strengths to seize opportunities), WO (fix weaknesses by using opportunities), ST (use strengths to counter threats), WT (minimize weaknesses and avoid threats). **SOAR** (strengths, opportunities, aspirations, results) is an appreciative-inquiry alternative (noted by `change`).
- Confusables: PESTLE (owned by `change`; external only, so it feeds SWOT's O and T); force field analysis (owned by `change`; driving vs restraining forces); the nine-box grid and BCG matrix (other grids).
- Unverified: origin and dates; TOWS author and year. The exam's likely testable point is the internal/external split, which is uncontested.

### Balanced scorecard
- Origin: Robert S. Kaplan and David P. Norton, "The Balanced Scorecard: Measures That Drive Performance", *Harvard Business Review*, January–February 1992, pp. 71–79. [S9] Later books: *The Balanced Scorecard* (1996) and *Strategy Maps* (2004) (general knowledge).
- Tie to the exam: 3.4.4 (a strategic planning and management tool) and 3.4.3 (aligning measures to strategy). In the ATD glossary [S7]; not in the statements or the reading list.
- Suggested tag: extra (ATD glossary only; not named in a statement). Use the glossary's names.
- Kind: set (four perspectives; the glossary's order differs from Kaplan and Norton's)
- Items, ATD glossary names and order [S7] ("customer, learning and growth (or innovation), internal business processes, and financial"):
  1. Customer.
  2. Learning and growth (or innovation).
  3. Internal business processes.
  4. Financial.
- Original 1992 names and questions [S9]:
  - Customer: "How do customers see us?"
  - Internal business: "What must we excel at?"
  - Innovation & learning: "Can we continue to improve and create value?"
  - Financial: "How do we look to shareholders?"
- ATD glossary purpose: a strategic planning and management system that tracks organizational performance through these four perspectives (paraphrase). [S7]
- Variants or contested points: **perspective names changed.** 1992: "internal business" and "innovation and learning" [S9]; the later standard names (1996 book onward, general knowledge) are "internal business process(es)" and "learning and growth". ATD's glossary uses the later names and notes the older one in parentheses: "learning and growth (or innovation)". [S7] Use **Customer, Internal business processes, Learning and growth, Financial** in questions, and accept that "innovation and learning" is the same perspective. The order also differs: Kaplan and Norton usually start with Financial; ATD's glossary starts with Customer. Never key a question on the order. Count is four in every version (some public-sector adaptations add or rename perspectives, general knowledge).
- TD's usual home: learning and growth (employee capability, skills, information systems, culture) is where TD measures usually sit (general knowledge).
- Confusables: Kirkpatrick's four levels (`kirk-levels`; also four items, also "balanced"); the TDRp measures (`tdrp-measures`: efficiency, effectiveness, outcome); strategy maps (Kaplan and Norton's follow-on tool); KPIs and dashboards.
- Unverified: the 1996 naming and order; strategy-map details.

## 3.4.5: Anticipated constraints on TD initiatives

No ATD or TDBoK list of constraint types was found within budget (the ATD glossary has no "constraint" entry [S7]). What the statement itself names is solid; the rest is general knowledge.

### The statement's two named examples
- Origin: the statement itself. [S1]
- Tie to the exam: 3.4.5, named.
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: set
- Items, in the statement's own terms [S1] "for example resource deficiencies or lack of support":
  1. Resource deficiencies: not enough budget, people, time, tools or technology to deliver the initiative (paraphrase; the statement doesn't define it).
  2. Lack of support: missing sponsorship or buy-in from leaders, managers or other stakeholders (paraphrase).
- Confusables: organizational barriers (owned by `biz`, statement 3.2.8 "identifying, minimizing, and overcoming organizational barriers"); resistance to change (owned by `change`); risks (`risk-responses`). A constraint is a known limit to plan around; a risk is an uncertain event; a barrier is an obstacle to implementation. That three-way distinction is general knowledge, not ATD wording, so avoid "which term" questions on it.

### Common constraint categories for TD initiatives (thin)
- Tie to the exam: 3.4.5 (what "anticipated constraints or problems" means in practice).
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set
- Items (general knowledge, unverified; group them under the statement's two examples):
  - Resource deficiencies: budget/funding; staff or SME availability; time (deadlines, learners' time away from work); technology and infrastructure (LMS limits, bandwidth, devices); facilities.
  - Lack of support: no executive sponsor; managers who don't release learners or reinforce learning; competing priorities; unclear link to business goals; low learner motivation; organizational culture.
  - Other: regulatory or legal requirements; geographic spread and time zones; language and accessibility needs; organizational change (restructuring, mergers).
- Ways to anticipate them (general knowledge): SWOT (weaknesses, threats) during planning; stakeholder analysis (see `biz`); force field analysis (see `change`: restraining forces); risk register and responses (`risk-responses`); a project charter's assumptions and constraints section (`charter`).
- Unverified: all categories. No ATD list was seen.

### Triple constraint (project management triangle)
- Origin: project-management tradition, often credited to Martin Barnes (1969, "iron triangle"); general knowledge, no source fetched.
- Tie to the exam: 3.4.5 (constraints on TD projects; 3.4.4 also says "projects"). Not in the statements, reading list or glossary [S7]. General knowledge only.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set (three linked constraints, with quality in the middle)
- Items (general knowledge): 1. Scope; 2. Time (schedule); 3. Cost (budget). Changing one forces a change in at least one other, or in quality.
- Variants or contested points: some versions put **quality** as a corner instead of the centre, or use "good, fast, cheap"; PMI's later guides list six or more competing constraints (scope, quality, schedule, budget, resources, risk). Count differs by version, so don't key on "three" without naming the triangle.
- Confusables: `pm-phases` and `charter` (already in the app); `risk-responses`.
- Unverified: Barnes attribution; PMI's constraint list.
