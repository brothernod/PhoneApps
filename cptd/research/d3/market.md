### ATD's six-step marketing process (Infoline "Marketing Training Programs")
- Origin: "Marketing Training Programs", ASTD Infoline / ATD TD at Work Guide, February 2001. [S4]
- Tie to the exam: 3.4.6 (establishing and executing a marketing strategy). ATD's own publication on marketing training, but not on the 2025 reading list [S3] and not shown in TDBoK text.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: ordered (a process, start to finish; ATD says it "adapts the traditional ADDIE model" into six steps) [S4]
- Items, in the source's own terms (ATD's product description lists them in this order; it does not number them):
  1. Needs and situation analysis: find out what the client base needs and the situation the program is entering. [S4] "beginning with a needs and situation analysis"
  2. Strategy: decide the marketing strategy. [S4]
  3. Promotion: promote the program. [S4]
  4. Production: produce the program and its materials. [S4]
  5. Distribution: get the program to its audience. [S4]
  6. Evaluation: evaluate the result. [S4]
- Key idea ATD states: a training intervention's success often depends on "how well it is marketed to your client base." [S4]
- Confusables: ADDIE (`addie`, five phases; this is a six-step marketing adaptation of it, so don't map the steps one to one); the strategic marketing planning process below [S7]; the 4 Ps (Promotion and Distribution here are close to the 4 Ps' Promotion and Place, which invites mix-ups); the PM phases (`pm-phases`).
- Variants or contested points: the descriptions read do not explain each step; the one-line meanings above are inferred from the step names only.
- Unverified: what each step contains inside the issue (paywalled); whether the issue also uses the 4 Ps; author.

### Strategic marketing planning process (Principles of HRD, seven steps)
- Origin: ASTD *Training and Development Journal*, September 1987, excerpt from *Principles of HRD* (probably Gilley and Eggland; see [S7]). Figure 1 of the excerpt. [S7]
- Tie to the exam: 3.4.6 ("establishing ... a marketing strategy"). ASTD-published; not on the reading list [S3]; not shown in TDBoK text.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: ordered (planning sequence; the last step, the marketing plan, "comprises all six previous steps") [S7]
- Items, in the source's own terms:
  1. Mission: what the HRD program exists to do, framed by three dimensions (below). [S7]
  2. Environmental analysis: scan the conditions the program operates in. [S7]
  3. Objectives and goals: set what the marketing effort must achieve. [S7]
  4. Target market determinants: decide which segments to serve (segmentation and targeting). [S7]
  5. Marketing mix: set the four Ps (product, place, promotion, price). [S7]
  6. Marketing strategy: choose the strategic decision (build, hold, harvest or divest). [S7]
  7. Marketing plan: the written plan that pulls the six earlier steps together. [S7] "A marketing plan comprises all six previous steps"
- Sub-lists in the same source:
  - **Two principles** behind all HRD marketing: customer service and fair exchange. [S7] "Two principles legitimize all successful HRD marketing efforts"
  - **Mission's three dimensions**: user groups (the clients to be served), user needs, technologies. [S7] "consider user groups—the clients to be served and satisfied"
  - **Strategy decisions (four)**: build, hold, harvest, divest. [S7] "to build, to hold, to harvest, and to divest"
- Confusables: ATD's six-step process above [S4]; SWOT and strategic planning steps (owned by `strategy`); PESTLE/environmental scanning (owned by `change`; "environmental analysis" here is the same idea used for marketing); the BCG growth-share matrix (build/hold/harvest/divest are the BCG strategy options, general knowledge).
- Variants or contested points: author names garbled in the scan; the strategy decisions match the BCG portfolio options, which the excerpt does not credit (as read).
- Unverified: authors; exact step wording beyond the figure labels the fetch tool returned.

### Marketing mix: the 4 Ps (as ASTD's HRD source gives it)
- Origin: E. Jerome McCarthy first proposed the 4 Ps in 1960 (popularized by Philip Kotler) [S11]; the book title, *Basic Marketing*, is general knowledge. The term "marketing mix" was popularized by Neil Borden (used from the late 1940s, 1953 AMA address), after James Culliton's 1948 "mixers of ingredients" [S11]. ASTD applied the 4 Ps to HRD in the 1987 *Principles of HRD* excerpt. [S7]
- Tie to the exam: 3.4.6. Only ATD-published HRD source read that lists a marketing mix is [S7]. Not on the reading list [S3]; TDBoK wording not seen. ATD's 2001 marketing guide [S4] and the 2025 ATD book [S10] show no Ps in the parts read.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set (no meaningful order; [S7] lists them product, place, promotion, price)
- Items, in the source's own terms:
  1. Product: the HRD program or service itself (what is offered to meet a user need). [S7]
  2. Place: where and how the program is delivered or made available (distribution). [S7]
  3. Promotion: how users are told about and persuaded to use it; [S7] splits it into personal selling, mass selling (advertising and public relations) and sales promotion (below).
  4. Price: in HRD, often not money but what the learner gives up: time, energy and commitment (the exchange). [S7]
- Key line: "A successful marketing mix requires all four Ps." [S7]
- Confusables: the 7 Ps and 4 Cs (next entries); ATD's six-step marketing process [S4] (its "Promotion" and "Distribution" steps resemble Promotion and Place); the 4 Ps' "Promotion" vs the promotion components list; Cialdini's principles (`cialdini`) and the value proposition (`value-prop`), which are influence/messaging tools, not the mix.
- Variants or contested points: see the 7 Ps and 4 Cs entries. **Which version ATD/the TDBoK uses could not be confirmed**; the only ATD-published HRD source read uses **four** Ps [S7]. A 5th P (People) appears in some training-marketing writing (general knowledge, unverified).
- Unverified: TDBoK treatment; McCarthy's original text (date and names via the encyclopedia [S11] only).

### HRD promotion components (three)
- Origin: *Principles of HRD* excerpt, ASTD journal, 1987. [S7]
- Tie to the exam: 3.4.6 ("promote talent development"). ASTD-published, not reading list.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set
- Items:
  1. Personal selling: one-to-one promotion (for example, talking a program through with a manager). [S7]
  2. Mass selling: reaching many users at once; it includes advertising and public relations. [S7] "Mass selling communicates with many users at the same time"
  3. Sales promotion: short-term incentives or devices to prompt use (paraphrase; the excerpt's own definition was not quoted). [S7]
- Confusables: the 4 Ps' Promotion (this list is the breakdown of that one P); the general "promotion mix" in marketing texts adds direct marketing and others (general knowledge).
- Unverified: [S7]'s definition of sales promotion.

### Target-market approaches (segmentation and coverage, six)
- Origin: *Principles of HRD* excerpt, Figure 2, ASTD journal, 1987. [S7]
- Tie to the exam: 3.4.6 (segmentation is part of a marketing strategy). ASTD-published.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set (in marketing texts these run roughly from least to most selective, but [S7] as read gives no order rule; the order below is the fetch tool's)
- Items, in the source's own terms:
  1. Mass marketing: one offering for everyone, no segmentation. [S7]
  2. Product-market concentration: one product for one segment. [S7]
  3. Product specialization: one product offered to several segments. [S7]
  4. Market specialization: several products for one segment. [S7]
  5. Selective specialization: chosen product–segment pairs; it "custom tailors the mix between products and segments". [S7]
  6. Full coverage: all products to all segments. [S7]
- Confusables: learner personas and learner segments (below) [S8][S9]; general segmentation bases (demographic, geographic, psychographic, behavioral; general knowledge, not from an ATD source); stakeholder mapping (owned by `biz`), which sorts stakeholders by power/interest, not by learning need.
- Variants or contested points: these six are the Abell / Kotler target-market patterns (general knowledge); meanings 2–4 and 6 are inferred from the names and standard marketing usage, not quoted from [S7].
- Unverified: [S7]'s own definitions for items 1–4 and 6.

### HRD product life cycle (four stages)
- Origin: *Principles of HRD* excerpt, Figure 3, ASTD journal, 1987. [S7]
- Tie to the exam: 3.4.6 (part of the Product decision in a marketing strategy). ASTD-published.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: ordered (start of a program's life to its end)
- Items, in the source's own terms:
  1. Exposure: the program is new and being introduced. [S7] "evolves through a four-stage cycle"
  2. Acceptance: users take it up and demand grows. [S7]
  3. Maturity: demand levels off. [S7]
  4. Decline: demand falls; time to revise or retire. [S7]
- Confusables: the standard marketing product life cycle, which names the stages **introduction, growth, maturity, decline** (general knowledge). The HRD version's first two names differ (Exposure, Acceptance); use the source's terms if this source is the basis for a question. Also ADDIE phases; build/hold/harvest/divest decisions (above).
- Unverified: [S7]'s one-line meaning of each stage (inferred from names).

### Extended marketing mix: the 7 Ps (services)
- Origin: Bernard Booms and Mary Jo Bitner, 1981, extending McCarthy's 4 Ps for services. [S11]
- Tie to the exam: 3.4.6. **Not found in any ATD source read** [S3][S4][S7][S8][S10]. Included because training is a service and the brief asks for extended versions. General knowledge only for the exam tie.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set (the 4 Ps plus three)
- Items, in the source's own terms:
  1–4. Product, Price, Place, Promotion (McCarthy's four, 1960). [S11]
  5. People: the humans who take part in delivering the service (for TD: facilitators, coaches, L&D staff). [S11] "Human factors who participate in service delivery"
  6. Process: the procedures, mechanisms and flow of activities that deliver the service (for TD: enrollment, scheduling, delivery workflow). [S11]
  7. Physical evidence: the environment where the service happens and tangible cues of it, such as facilities and layout (for TD: the classroom, the LMS look, materials, certificates). [S11]
- Confusables: the 4 Ps (items 1–4); the 4 Cs; an "8 Ps" services version that adds performance (cited to Kotler 2012) [S11]; the four Ps in ATD's HRD excerpt [S7].
- Variants or contested points: the TD-flavored examples in parentheses are this file's illustrations, not from a source. Which mix version the TDBoK uses: **unknown**.
- Unverified: whether the TDBoK or any CPTD material mentions the 7 Ps at all.

### Customer-centered mix: Lauterborn's 4 Cs
- Origin: Robert Lauterborn, 1990, a "more consumer-orientated version of the 4 Ps". [S11]
- Tie to the exam: 3.4.6. **Not found in any ATD source read.** General knowledge only.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set (each C pairs with one P)
- Items, in the source's own terms (C → the P it replaces):
  1. Consumer (wants and needs) → Product. [S11]
  2. Cost (to the consumer) → Price. [S11] (Compare [S7]: in HRD, "price" is the learner's time, energy and commitment, which is close to Lauterborn's "cost".)
  3. Convenience → Place. [S11]
  4. Communication → Promotion. [S11]
- Confusables: **Shimizu's 4 Cs** (commodity, cost, channel, communication; 1973, later the 7 Cs Compass Model, 1979) [S11], a different 4 Cs with overlapping words; the 4 Ps and 7 Ps; the Domain 1 communication models (`comm-process`), since "Communication" here is just the counterpart of Promotion.
- Variants or contested points: the bracketed "(wants and needs)" and "(to the consumer)" glosses are standard usage, not quoted. Some secondary sources say "Customer" instead of "Consumer"; [S11] gives Consumer.
- Unverified: Lauterborn's original article wording (not fetched).

- Origin: ATD Research and the Institute for Corporate Productivity (i4cp), *Lifelong Learning: The Path to Personal and Organizational Performance*, published **August 2018** per ATD's report page [S17] (press release dated September 25, 2018 [S14]), sponsored by OpenSesame [S14].   6. Building a learning culture is a high-performance practice linked to better market performance and learning effectiveness. [S14]

  5. Linking **internal mobility** options to continuous learning (9%).
  6. **Compensation** programs tied to continuous learning (7%; none of the lower performers do it).

  1. Better organizational performance.
  2. Improved talent retention.
  3. Greater ability to respond to changing business needs.
  4. Greater competitive ability.
- Benefits in i4cp's words [S18]: engagement "and by extension, better individual and organizational performance"; employees who "stay relevant, in demand" and can drive career growth; "competitive success in a fluid business world"; a vibrant learning culture.
