## 3.5.1: Theories, models and principles of HPI

### Definition and principles of HPI (ATD)
- Origin: ATD's HPI program pages and articles [S4][S5].
- Tie to the exam: the core of 3.5.1. ATD's own definitions; HPI is the subject of the reading-list book *Performance Basics* [S3][S6]. Not shown in the TDBoK.
- Suggested tag: reflist (the concept is the subject of a reading-list book; the wording here is from ATD articles, not the book)
- Kind: set (principles)
- Definition: HPI is "a results-based systematic process" used to identify, analyze and address workplace performance problems [S4]. ATD's 2017 article calls HPI "results based and systematic" and contrasts it with approaches driven by wants or needs [S5].
- Principles named in [S5] (labels as returned by the fetch tool; the article's exact headings were not quoted, so recheck before using them as option text):
  1. Results-based: driven by a business need and a performance need, and justified by what cause analysis finds. [S5]
  2. Business-focused: starts from the organization's strategic priorities and key business goals. [S5]
  3. Measurable: goals are quantitative and time-limited, so success can be measured. [S5]
  4. Top-down: analysis starts with business output and works back to departments, teams and individuals. [S5]
- Performance gap: the difference between current and desired performance [S5].
- ATD's list of performance enablers to look at, beyond skills: motivation, managerial support, technology, resources, organizational structure, process [S4]. A 2022 ATD post groups causes under four headings: Workplace Structure, Manager Support, Appropriate Skills or Knowledge, Motivation [S8] (headings from the text beside a figure of the "ATD Human Performance Model" that could not be read).
- Confusables: training needs analysis (HPI asks whether training is the answer at all; ATD stresses solutions "that may not be training" [S4]); performance management (appraisal cycle, owned by `strategy`); ISPI's HPT (same field, different body; see below).
- Unverified: the exact headings in [S5]; whether the TDBoK lists the same principles.

### ATD's HPI model (process steps)
- Origin: ASTD/ATD. First published as the ASTD HPI model by W. Rothwell, *ASTD Models for Human Performance Improvement* (ASTD, 1996; 2nd ed. 2000) (general knowledge; not fetched). ATD's current HPI Certificate teaches "ATD's practical HPI model" [S4].
- Tie to the exam: 3.5.1 (and the process behind 3.5.4 and 3.5.5). ATD's own model; *Performance Basics* (reading list) teaches HPI and its Performance DNA analysis [S3][S6]. Not shown in the TDBoK.
- Suggested tag: reflist (weak: the book's chapter list could not be read; see Unverified)
- Kind: ordered, first to last (a cycle: evaluation feeds back into analysis)
- Items, current ATD version, as ATD's HPI Certificate words what participants learn to do [S4] (ATD gives these as verb phrases, not phase names):
  1. Articulate business goals. [S4]
  2. Link business goals to human performance. [S4]
  3. Diagnose an organization's current state of performance. [S4]
  4. Identify root causes of performance gaps. [S4]
  5. Implement appropriate solutions, which "may not be training". [S4]
  6. Evaluate results. [S4]
  7. Increase impact through effective change management. [S4]
  The 2017 ATD article gives the same sequence as six steps (no separate change-management step) [S5].
- Older ASTD version (six steps, as presented on [S9], which credits ASTD and cites a 2000 *Info-line*): 1 Performance Analysis (gap between actual and desired) → 2 Cause Analysis (root causes) → 3 Intervention Selection → 4 Intervention Implementation → 5 Change Management → 6 Evaluation of Results. [S9]
- Groups: ATD's current wording puts a business step first (steps 1–2 = business analysis) before performance analysis (step 3). The older ASTD version starts at Performance Analysis.
- Confusables: ADDIE (`addie`; a design model, not a performance model: HPI selects among many solutions, of which training is one); ISPI's HPT model (Van Tiem et al.; same shape, different phase names); Performance DNA (ATD's analysis method that covers only the front end); Robinson's performance consulting process; consulting phases (owned by `biz`).
- Variants or contested points: phase names and counts differ by source and edition. Commonly cited names for the ASTD model in later ATD materials are Business Analysis, Performance Analysis, Cause Analysis, Intervention (Solution) Selection and Design, Implementation and Change Management, Evaluation (general knowledge; not confirmed in any tier 1–3 source this session). Rothwell's 1996 original is usually given as Performance Analysis, Cause Analysis, Intervention Selection, Implementation, Change Management, Evaluation and Measurement (general knowledge). What ATD uses now: [S4] and [S5] both start from business goals, so the current ATD version has a business-analysis front step; in [S4] change management comes last, in the older version it sits between implementation and evaluation. Don't test exact phase labels or the count; test the sequence (business goals → performance gap → root cause → solution → implement → evaluate).
- Unverified: the official phase labels of the current ATD model (the ATD figure in [S8] is an image); *Performance Basics* 2nd ed. chapter titles; whether the TDBoK prints the model.

### Performance DNA (ATD's front-end analysis method)
- Origin: ATD's HPI and Analyzing Human Performance Certificate programs; used in Willmore's *Performance Basics*, 2nd ed. ("See the Performance DNA process you know from ATD's Human Performance Improvement program") [S6][S7].
- Tie to the exam: 3.5.4 (performance analysis) and 3.5.1. Reading-list book [S3][S6].
- Suggested tag: reflist
- Kind: ordered, first to last
- Items [S7] (each phase "aligns with ATD's HPI Model"):
  1. Business Analysis: clarify the sponsor's request, define desired results, identify key roles, write a summary project plan. [S7]
  2. Performance Analysis: identify the performance gaps to fill and document "outcomes of value". [S7]
  3. Key Performer Analysis: how the work gets done, and what lets exemplary performers outperform standard ones. [S7]
  4. Influence Analysis: an in-depth check of positive and negative influences on performance, to confirm or reject hypotheses. [S7]
- [S7] calls it a systematic "front end" analysis method; it uses structured observation and interviewing.
- Confusables: the ATD HPI model (Performance DNA is only its analysis front end, with no solution or evaluation phases); Gilbert's exemplary vs typical performer (PIP, the same idea as Key Performer Analysis); Rummler's levels; Robinson's need types.
- Unverified: whether the book uses identical phase names (the product page names "Performance DNA" only [S6]).

### Gilbert's Behavior Engineering Model (BEM)
- Origin: Thomas F. Gilbert, *Human Competence: Engineering Worthy Performance*, 1978; tribute edition Pfeiffer/ISPI 2007 [S10].
- Tie to the exam: 3.5.1 (HPI models) and 3.5.4 (cause analysis). Only general knowledge ties it to ATD here: neither reading-list book's text was read, and the TDBoK was not seen. It is the classic HPI cause-analysis model and appears in nearly all HPI materials.
- Suggested tag: extra (raise to reflist or tdbok only if a later pass sees it in *Performance Basics* or the TDBoK)
- Kind: set (a 2 × 3 matrix), with a recommended diagnostic order (environment row first, see below)
- Structure [S10]: built from Gilbert's formula P = B × E (behavior and environment) crossed with the ABC sequence (antecedents, behaviors, consequences). Six variables: information, resources, incentives, knowledge, capacity, motives. [S10]
- Items, in the usual layout (row and cell placement is general knowledge; [S10] lists the six variables but not their cells):
  - Environment row ("environmental supports"):
    1. Information (Gilbert's own cell label: Data): clear expectations, guidance, timely feedback. Antecedent column.
    2. Resources (Gilbert: Instruments): tools, time, materials, processes designed to fit the work. Behavior column.
    3. Incentives: financial and non-financial rewards and consequences tied to performance. Consequence column.
  - Individual row ("person's repertory of behavior"):
    4. Knowledge (later versions: Knowledge and Skills): what the person knows and can do; training fixes this cell. Antecedent column.
    5. Capacity: the person's ability to perform (physical, mental, emotional fit; selection). Behavior column.
    6. Motives: the person's willingness to perform. Consequence column.
- Order and leverage: Gilbert held that the biggest barrier to exemplary performance is "absence of performance support at work", not a lack of individual knowledge or skill, so look at the environment row before the individual row [S10]. The usual teaching order is Information → Resources → Incentives → then the individual row, with training (Knowledge) considered last (general knowledge).
- Groups: Environment = Information, Resources, Incentives. Individual = Knowledge, Capacity, Motives. Pairs by column: Information/Knowledge, Resources/Capacity, Incentives/Motives.
- Related Gilbert concepts (general knowledge, not fetched): worthy performance W = A/B (value of the accomplishment over the cost of the behavior); Potential for Improving Performance, PIP = exemplary performance ÷ typical performance (the bigger the ratio, the more room to improve); both come from his "leisurely theorems".
- Confusables: Rummler's Human Performance System (inputs, performer, outputs, consequences, feedback), which covers similar factors as a loop, not a grid; Mager–Pipe (flowchart, not grid); ATD's enablers list [S4] and the four headings in [S8]; Maslow (`maslow`) for "motives". Don't confuse "Incentives" (environment) with "Motives" (individual), or "Resources" (environment) with "Capacity" (individual): the most likely wrong answers.
- Variants or contested points: cell names differ by version. Gilbert 1978: Data, Instruments, Incentives / Knowledge, Capacity, Motives (general knowledge; [S10] uses the modern words information and resources). R. Chevalier's "updated BEM" (2003, *Performance Improvement* journal) uses Information, Resources, Incentives / Knowledge and Skills, Capacity, Motives and recommends working through the individual row in reverse (Motives, Capacity, then Knowledge and Skills) (general knowledge; not fetched). What ATD uses: not seen this session. Use the modern names (Information, Resources, Incentives, Knowledge, Capacity, Motives), which [S10] also uses, and don't test Data or Instruments as the only right answer. The often-quoted claim that most performance problems (75–80%) lie in the environment row is not sourced here; don't test a number.
- Unverified: the cell placement and column names from the book itself; Chevalier's order; whether the TDBoK or *Performance Basics* prints the BEM.

### Rummler and Brache's three levels of performance (and nine performance variables)
- Origin: Geary A. Rummler and Alan P. Brache, *Improving Performance: How to Manage the White Space on the Organization Chart*, Jossey-Bass; [S11] cites the 2012 edition (3rd). First edition 1990, 2nd 1995 (general knowledge).
- Tie to the exam: 3.5.1 (HPI models) and 3.5.7 (analysis of systems). Only general knowledge ties it to ATD; not seen in the reading-list books or the TDBoK this session.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: a 3 × 3 matrix. The levels are ordered top to bottom (organization → process → job/performer); the needs are a set.
- Items [S11]:
  - Three levels: 1. Organization; 2. Process; 3. Job/Performer.
  - Three performance needs: Goals; Design; Management.
  - Nine performance variables: each level crossed with each need (Organization Goals, Organization Design, Organization Management; Process Goals, Process Design, Process Management; Job/Performer Goals, Design and Management). [S11]
- Meanings (general knowledge): Organization level = the organization's strategy, its relationship with its market and how its major functions fit. Process level = the cross-functional workflows that produce products and services; the "white space" between boxes on the org chart, where Rummler says most improvement lies. Job/Performer level = the individuals doing the work. Goals = the standards each level must meet; Design = how the level is structured to meet them; Management = the practices that keep goals and design current and working.
- Confusables: Kirkpatrick's levels (`kirk-levels`; evaluation, not performance); Kaufman's Mega/Macro/Micro (societal/organizational/individual results); Gilbert's BEM (factors behind individual performance, which mostly sits at Rummler's third level); ATD's HPI model's move from business goals to human performance; open systems theory (owned by `od`).
- Variants or contested points: the third level is named "Job/Performer" in [S11]; other sources say "Job", "Performer" or "Individual" level. Accept any; don't build a question on the label. "Goals, Design, Management" is consistent across what was seen.
- Unverified: meanings above and the "white space" claim come from general knowledge, not the book.

### Rummler's human performance system (job/performer level)
- Origin: Rummler & Brache, *Improving Performance* (job/performer level chapter). General knowledge only; not fetched.
- Tie to the exam: 3.5.1 and 3.5.7. General knowledge.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set (a loop)
- Items (general knowledge): 1. Inputs (the job situation: triggers, materials, resources); 2. Performer; 3. Outputs; 4. Consequences (what follows the output for the performer); 5. Feedback (information back to the performer on how they did).

### Mager and Pipe's performance analysis flowchart
- Origin: Robert F. Mager and Peter Pipe, *Analyzing Performance Problems, or You Really Oughta Wanna*. [S12] attributes the model to Mager and Pipe (1984). Editions 1970, 1984 (2nd), 1997 (3rd) (general knowledge).
- Tie to the exam: 3.5.1 and 3.5.4 (cause analysis). General knowledge; Mager is named in Domain 2 (2.1.3) for a different work.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: ordered (a yes/no decision flowchart [S12]; [S12] notes real problems are "more complex and nuanced than a simple 'yes' or 'no.'")
- Items, as usually given for the 1997 edition (general knowledge; [S12]'s flowchart is an image and could not be read):
  1. What is the problem? (whose performance; what is the discrepancy)
  2. Is it worth pursuing? (cost of the problem vs cost of solving it)
  3. Can we apply fast fixes? (are expectations clear, are resources adequate, is performance quality visible)
  4. Are consequences appropriate? (is desired performance punishing, is poor performance rewarding, are consequences used effectively)
  5. Do they already know how? (is there a genuine skill deficiency; could they do it in the past; is the skill used often)
  6. Are there more clues? (can the task be simplified, are there other obstacles, does the person have the potential to change)
  7. Select and implement solutions (cost the options, pick the best, implement)
- Key idea (general knowledge): separate "can't do" (a real skill deficiency, fixed with training, practice, feedback or job aids) from everything else (expectations, resources, consequences, obstacles). Training is chosen only after the non-training causes are ruled out. The classic test question is whether the person could do it if their life depended on it.
- Confusables: Mager's three-part objectives (d2 `design`; performance, conditions, criterion) and his Criterion-Referenced Instruction (2.1.3); Gilbert's BEM (grid vs flowchart); Harless's front-end analysis (general knowledge; [S9]'s site has a separate "Harless Model" page, not fetched).
- Variants or contested points: the 1970 and 1984 editions use a longer flowchart with different wording; the question count and labels vary by edition. Don't test exact wording or the number of steps. The order "fast fixes and consequences before skill deficiency" is the testable point.
- Unverified: all step wording.

## 3.5.4: Performance analysis to identify goals, gaps, or opportunities

Statement terms: goals, gaps, opportunities. The APTD-only 3.5.2 [S2] lists the methods the TDBoK groups here: business process analysis, performance gap assessment, root-cause analysis. See 3.5.1 for Performance DNA (ATD's four-phase analysis method), the BEM and Mager–Pipe, which are the main cause-analysis frameworks.

### Gap analysis (desired vs actual performance)
- Origin: standard HPI practice. ATD: a performance gap is the difference between current and desired performance levels, and the analysis starts from business analysis [S5]. The older ASTD model's Performance Analysis step "identifies the gap between actual and desired performance" (paraphrase) [S9].
- Tie to the exam: named in 3.5.4 ("gaps") and 3.5.5 ("performance gaps"). 3.5.2 names "performance gap assessment" [S2].
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: ordered (general practice)
- Items (sequence from [S5][S9]; step wording is mine):
  1. Define desired performance from business goals (the "should").
  2. Measure actual (current) performance (the "is").
  3. Describe the gap: its size, who it affects and what it costs.
  4. Pass the gap to cause analysis before choosing any solution. [S4][S5]
- The three objects in 3.5.4 (my reading; ATD's definitions not seen): goals = the desired state the analysis starts from; gaps = current shortfalls against that state (problems, reactive); opportunities = a desired future state not yet reached, where no current problem exists (proactive).
- Confusables: needs assessment vs training needs analysis (a TNA assumes training is the answer; HPI gap analysis doesn't, [S4]); McGehee and Thayer's three levels of training needs analysis (organization, task/operations, person; general knowledge); Kaufman's view that a need is a gap in results, not in means (general knowledge); Rossett's "optimals and actuals" (general knowledge). SWOT (owned by `strategy`) also finds opportunities, but for strategy, not performance.
- Variants or contested points: the pair is worded desired/actual, optimal/actual, should/is or desired/current depending on source. All mean the same.
- Unverified: ATD's own definitions of goal, gap and opportunity.

### Robinson and Robinson's types of needs (Performance Consulting)
- Origin: Dana Gaines Robinson and James C. Robinson, *Performance Consulting* (1995; 2nd ed. 2008). The 2015 edition on ATD's list, *Performance Consulting: A Strategic Process to Improve, Measure, and Sustain Organizational Results*, adds Jack and Patti Phillips (and D. Handshaw, general knowledge) [S3].
- Tie to the exam: 3.5.4. The book is on the reading list under 3.5 [S3]; the items below were not seen in it.
- Suggested tag: reflist for the book; the item list is general knowledge, so authors should treat it as extra until a gap fill confirms it.
- Kind: ordered, top-down (business first)
- Items (general knowledge, 2nd ed. terms):
  1. Business needs: the unit's or organization's operational goals, in numbers. Either business problems (a current gap) or business opportunities (a future goal with no current problem).
  2. Performance needs: what people must do on the job to meet the business goals.
  3. Capability needs (learning needs): the skills and knowledge people need for that performance.
  4. Work environment needs: the systems and processes around the performer (clear roles, incentives, tools, coaching).
- Related (general knowledge): the problem vs opportunity split matches 3.5.4's "gaps, or opportunities"; Robinson's "reactive vs proactive" performance consulting (responding to a training request vs finding opportunities); the GAPS! map (named only; items not recalled reliably).
- Confusables: Phillips's needs levels in the V-model (payoff, business, performance, learning, preference needs; see `phillips-levels` and d2 `eval`), which the 2015 edition likely ties to; Rummler's levels; Performance DNA; BEM's environment vs individual rows (work environment vs capability needs).
- Variants or contested points: "capability" vs "learning" needs differs between sources and editions. The 2015 edition's own terms were not seen.
- Unverified: all item names and the GAPS! map. High priority for a gap fill, since this is the reading-list book.

### Cause analysis (root-cause analysis) and its tools
- Origin: the HPI step after performance analysis [S4][S5][S9]. In the ASTD model, cause analysis looks for root causes at the organization, process and individual levels [S9]. Tools are general knowledge: Ishikawa (fishbone, cause-and-effect) diagram; the 5 whys (Toyota); Pareto analysis; Gilbert's BEM and Mager–Pipe as frameworks.
- Tie to the exam: 3.5.4 (the APTD 3.5.2 names "root-cause analysis" [S2]) and 3.5.1. ATD's HPI step "Identify root causes of performance gaps" [S4].
- Suggested tag: reflist for cause analysis as an HPI step; extra for the named tools
- Kind: set (tools)
- Items (tools; general knowledge):
  1. Fishbone (Ishikawa) diagram: the problem at the head, possible causes grouped by category on the bones. Category sets vary (a common one: people, methods, machines, materials, measurement, environment).
  2. 5 whys: ask "why?" repeatedly (about five times) until the root cause shows.
  3. Pareto analysis: rank causes by frequency or impact, the "80/20" view.
  4. Cause frameworks: Gilbert's BEM, Mager–Pipe, Rummler's levels (see 3.5.1).
  5. Data sources: interviews, focus groups, observation, surveys, existing performance data (data methods owned by `data`).
- Key distinctions: symptom vs root cause; environmental vs individual causes (BEM rows); can't do vs won't do (Mager–Pipe).
- Confusables: gap analysis (what and how big) vs cause analysis (why); fault-tree analysis; a "performance enabler" list [S4].
- Variants or contested points: fishbone category sets differ (6 Ms, 4 Ps, 4 Ss); don't test a fixed category list.
- Unverified: the tools' details (general knowledge).

### Types of analysis covered in *Performance Basics*
- Origin: the product page of Willmore's *Performance Basics*, 2nd ed., lists the analysis types the book addresses: front-end analysis, accomplishment-focused analysis, root cause analysis, and formative, summative and ROI analyses [S6].
- Tie to the exam: 3.5.4 (and 3.5.1). Reading-list book [S3].
- Suggested tag: reflist
- Kind: set, in two groups
- Items [S6], with meanings from general knowledge:
  1. Front-end analysis: analysis done before any solution is chosen, to confirm a problem worth solving and its causes (term associated with Joe Harless).
  2. Accomplishment-focused analysis: looks at what the work produces (outputs, accomplishments) rather than at behavior (Gilbert's distinction between accomplishment and behavior).
  3. Root cause analysis: see above.
  4. Formative analysis (evaluation): done during development or rollout, to improve the solution.
  5. Summative analysis (evaluation): done afterwards, to judge results.
  6. ROI analysis: monetary benefits against costs (`roi-formulas`, `phillips-levels`).
- Groups: before the solution = 1–3; after = 4–6.

## 3.5.5: Designing and developing performance improvement solutions

### Selecting performance improvement solutions (interventions)
- Origin: ATD's HPI process [S4]; the ASTD model's Intervention Selection step, which chooses the interventions that best address the root causes, weighs success, cost and obstacles, and records the choice in a design document [S9].
- Tie to the exam: 3.5.5 (the statement's subject). ATD course "Selecting and Implementing Performance Improvement Solutions" exists (title seen in search only; not fetched).
- Suggested tag: named (the statement names "performance improvement solutions"; the steps come from ATD and [S9], not the TDBoK)
- Kind: ordered (steps), with a set of criteria
- Items, steps (from [S4][S9]; wording mine):
  1. Start from the root causes found in cause analysis; each cause needs a matching solution. Training fits only a knowledge or skill cause. [S4]
  2. List candidate solutions, training and non-training.
  3. Weigh them on likely success, cost and obstacles. [S9]
  4. Choose a set of solutions and record it in a design document. [S9]
  5. Plan implementation and change management, including a respected sponsor. [S9] (ATD: "Increase impact through effective change management" [S4])
  6. Plan the evaluation of results against the original business goal. [S4][S9]
- Other common criteria (general knowledge): time to implement, sponsor and stakeholder support, fit with the culture, available resources, sustainability.
- Confusables: ADDIE (designs one training solution; `addie`); the business case (`biz`); build vs buy (`build-buy`).

### Gottfredson and Mosher's Five Moments of Need
- Origin: Conrad Gottfredson and Bob Mosher; [S13] cites their 2011 book *Innovative Performance Support* (publisher McGraw-Hill, general knowledge).
- Tie to the exam: 3.5.6. Not on ATD's reading list [S3]; Dillon's reading-list book cites it as an inspiration (d2 `techkm`). Only general knowledge ties it to the TDBoK.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set with a standard order (the usual numbering 1–5 as below); grouped
- Items, in the authors' labels as given by [S13]:
  1. New: learning something for the first time [S13] "learning something for the first time"
  2. More: deepening knowledge or refining skills [S13]
  3. Apply: using what was learned on the job [S13] "apply what they have learned in real-world scenarios"
  4. Solve: troubleshooting when something goes wrong or is unexpected [S13]
  5. Change: adapting when processes, policies or technology change [S13]
- Groups: New and More are the learning moments, where formal training (ILT, e-learning, courses, coaching) fits; Apply, Solve and Change are workflow moments, where performance support (job aids, checklists, searchable resources, FAQs, expert help) fits. [S13] implies this split; it says traditional training focuses on the first two.
- Confusables: 70-20-10 (`ccl-70-20-10`); the MLE layers; Kolb's cycle; the "Change" moment vs change management models (owned by `change`): the moment is about an individual's work method changing, including unlearning the old way; the "Solve" moment is about problems, not about learning more.
- Variants or contested points: also called the "five moments of learning need". Gottfredson and Mosher are often quoted as saying Apply is the most critical moment (general knowledge; not seen). The authors' own definitions were not read; [S13] is a college summary.
- Unverified: the authors' exact definitions; publication history before 2011.

### Electronic performance support system (EPSS) (Gery)
- Origin: Gloria Gery, *Electronic Performance Support Systems* (1991). General knowledge; not fetched.
- Tie to the exam: 3.5.6 ("performance support systems"). General knowledge.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: definition plus a set of types
- Definition (paraphrase of Gery's, general knowledge): an integrated electronic environment that gives each worker immediate, individualized, on-demand access to information, tools, advice, data and learning, so they can do the job with minimal help from other people.
- Types of support (Gery's later classification, general knowledge):
  1. Intrinsic: built into the work tool or interface itself, so the task needs no extra help (for example, a form that fills in known values).
  2. Extrinsic: integrated with the system but optional for the user (for example, context-sensitive help, wizards).
  3. External: outside the work system (for example, manuals, job aids, help desks, coaches, a separate knowledge base).
- Observation (mine): 3.5.6's examples (instructional resources, data, process models, job aids, expert advice) read like Gery's EPSS contents.
- Confusables: performance management system (PMS, appraisal) vs performance support system (d2 `techkm` already flags this); LMS; KMS; a job aid is the simplest external support.
- Unverified: the definition wording, the 1991 publisher, the three type names.

## 3.5.7: Analysis of systems to improve human performance

Statement's named examples, in printed order: determining how organizations learn, closing knowledge or skill gaps, addressing human factors issues. ### Performance as a product of the system (the HPI systems view)
- Origin: Gilbert's P = B × E (behavior and environment together) [S10]; Rummler and Brache's three levels [S11]; ASTD's cause analysis across organization, process and individual levels [S9].
- Tie to the exam: 3.5.7. HPI is systemic by definition [S4][S5].
- Suggested tag: extra (the concept; the parts are in the 3.5.1 entries)
- Kind: set (principles)
- Items:
  1. Look at the environment before the individual (Gilbert [S10]).
  2. Analyze all three levels: organization, process, job/performer [S11]. Most problems that look like people problems sit in the process level's white space (general knowledge, Rummler).
  3. Start from business output and work down to the performers (top-down, [S5]).
  4. A fix in one part of the system affects others; plan for change management and side effects [S4].
- Related (general knowledge, Rummler, often paraphrased): putting a good performer in a bad system usually lets the system win. Don't quote it as a verbatim quote.
- Confusables: open systems theory and systems thinking (`od`), which are the theory; this entry is the HPI application.
- Unverified: the paraphrased Rummler saying.

### Human factors (named in 3.5.7)
- Origin: the TDBoK's own human-factors statement is 3.5.3, "Knowledge of how human interactions with work environments, tools, equipment, and technology affect individual and organizational performance" [S2] (not tested on the CPTD, see the statement check).
- Tie to the exam: named in 3.5.7.
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: set
- Items: the interactions 3.5.3 names [S2]: 1. work environments; 2. tools; 3. equipment; 4. technology.
- Related (general knowledge): human factors (ergonomics) designs work to fit human abilities and limits, to cut error and improve performance and safety. Three common domains: physical (posture, lifting, workstation), cognitive (mental workload, attention, decision making, human error, usability), organizational (communication, teamwork, schedules, fatigue). Typical fixes: redesign the task or tool, error-proofing, checklists, better interfaces, before training the person.
- Confusables: Gilbert's Resources (environment) and Capacity (individual) cells; Rummler's task support; "human factors" is not "human resources" or "human capital".
- Unverified: the three-domain split (general knowledge, attributed to the International Ergonomics Association).

### How organizations learn: single- and double-loop learning (Argyris and Schön)
- Origin: Chris Argyris and Donald A. Schön, *Organizational Learning: A Theory of Action Perspective*, 1978. General knowledge; not fetched.
- Tie to the exam: 3.5.7 ("determining how organizations learn"). General knowledge.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: ordered (lower to higher order of learning)
- Items (general knowledge):
  1. Single-loop learning: detect and fix errors within the existing goals, norms and assumptions (the thermostat example).
  2. Double-loop learning: question and change the governing norms and assumptions themselves.
  3. Deutero-learning: learning how to learn (term from Bateson; "triple-loop" is a later extension, not Argyris and Schön's).
- Also in the app: `senge` (learning organization), `tacit-explicit` and `seci` (how knowledge moves and is created), MLE (d2 `techkm`).
- Confusables: Senge's five disciplines (mental models are close to double-loop questioning); Kolb's cycle (individual learning); action research (owned by `od`).
- Unverified: everything here.

### Closing knowledge or skill gaps (named in 3.5.7)
- Origin: the named example; no separate model. Uses the 3.5.1 and 3.5.4 tools.
- Tie to the exam: named in 3.5.7.
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: ordered (general practice; wording mine)
- Items:
  1. Confirm the gap really is knowledge or skill: Gilbert's Knowledge cell; Mager–Pipe's "Do they already know how?" (see 3.5.1). Otherwise fix the environment instead.
  2. Choose by need: training and practice for new skills (New and More moments); job aids and performance support for rare or complex tasks (Apply, Solve, Change); coaching for refinement.
