## 3.6.1: Change management theories and models

### Kotter's 8-step process for leading change
- Origin: John P. Kotter, "Leading Change: Why Transformation Efforts Fail", *Harvard Business Review*, 1995, expanded into the book *Leading Change* (Harvard Business School Press, 1996; new-preface edition 2012). [S3] [S5] (The 1995 article and 1996 date are general knowledge; [S5] cites only the book title.)
- Tie to the exam: named in 3.6.1 ("Kotter") [S1]; *Leading Change* (2012) is on ATD's reading list under 3.6 [S3].
- Suggested tag: reflist (named in the statement as well)
- Kind: ordered (the sequence in which a leader should drive a large change; [S5] numbers the steps and groups them in phases but does not say they must be strictly sequential)
- Items, in the source's own terms (Kotter International page [S5]):
  1. Create a sense of urgency: help others see why change is needed now. [S5] "Create a sense of urgency"
  2. Build a guiding coalition: assemble a group with enough power and credibility to lead the change. [S5] "Build a guiding coalition"
  3. Develop a vision and a strategy: say where the change is going and how. [S5] "Develop a vision and a strategy"
  4. Communicate widely and enlist more supporters: spread the vision so people buy in. [S5] "Communicate widely and enlist more supporters"
  5. Enable action by removing barriers: remove obstacles (structures, systems, people) that block the vision. [S5] "Enable action by removing barriers"
  6. Generate short-term wins: plan and celebrate visible early successes. [S5] "Generate short-term wins"
  7. Consolidate gains and sustain acceleration: use credibility from wins to drive more change; don't declare victory early. [S5] "Consolidate gains and sustain acceleration"
  8. Anchor new approaches in the culture to institute change: tie new behaviours to success so they stick. [S5] "Anchor new approaches in the culture to institute change"
- Groups (from [S5]): "Defrosting the status quo" → steps 1–4; "Introducing new practices" → steps 5–7; "Making it stick" → step 8. [S5]
- Confusables: Lewin's unfreeze–change–refreeze (Kotter's three groups map onto it: defrosting ≈ unfreeze, new practices ≈ change, making it stick ≈ refreeze); ADKAR (individual, not organizational, steps); Bridges (transition, not change); Kotter's eight *errors* (the mirror list in the same source, e.g. "Undercommunicating the vision by a factor of 10 (or 100 or even 1,000)" [S5]); Lewin's leadership styles (app id `lewin`, unrelated).
- Variants or contested points: the step names are worded differently in different Kotter publications, so questions should test the idea of each step, not one exact phrasing.
  - 1996 book wording (general knowledge, not seen in a fetched source this session): Establishing a sense of urgency; Creating the guiding coalition; Developing a vision and strategy; Communicating the change vision; Empowering broad-based action; Generating short-term wins; Consolidating gains and producing more change; Anchoring new approaches in the culture.
  - *The Heart of Change* (Kotter & Cohen, 2002) style wording, as narrated by Mind Tools [S6]: "creating a sense of urgency", "putting together a guiding team", "developing a change vision and strategy", "communicating the vision", "empowering others to act", "producing short-term wins", "don't let up", "making change stick". [S6]
  - Current Kotter Inc wording (kotterinc.com, 2026) [S7]: 1 "Create A Sense of Urgency", 2 "Build A Guiding Coalition", 3 "Form A Strategic Vision", 4 "Enlist A Volunteer Army", 5 "Enable Action By Removing Barriers", 6 "Generate Short-Term Wins", 7 "Sustain Acceleration", 8 "Institute Change". The firm says it "evolved the linear 8 Step" process from *Leading Change* into the "8 Accelerators" of *Accelerate* (2014) and the firm's book *CHANGE* (2021). [S7] So "volunteer army", "sustain acceleration" and "institute change" belong to the 2014-on version, not the 1996 book. The idea that the accelerators run concurrently and through a "dual operating system" is general knowledge about *Accelerate*; [S7] says only "linear" (old) vs "dynamic" (new).
  - Which version ATD uses: the reading list names *Leading Change* (2012 edition of the 1996 book) [S3], so the 1996 eight-stage process is the safest basis. Sequence matters in that version (Kotter warns skipping steps creates only an illusion of speed: general knowledge, not seen).
- Unverified: the exact 1996 step wording and the "skipping steps" warning (general knowledge only); [S5]'s page date. The often-quoted "70% of change efforts fail" figure was not found in any source here: don't use it.

### Lewin's change model (unfreeze, change, refreeze)
- Origin: Kurt Lewin, usually cited to "Frontiers in Group Dynamics", *Human Relations*, 1947 (general knowledge; no Lewin text fetched this session).
- Tie to the exam: 3.6.1 names "Lewin" with no model specified [S1]. In a change management statement this means the change model, not Lewin's leadership styles. ATD's reading list has no Lewin title [S3].
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: ordered (the three stages in time order)
- Items (standard wording, general knowledge, not seen in a tier 1–3 source this session):
  1. Unfreeze: create readiness by making the need for change felt and loosening the current equilibrium.
  2. Change (also called "moving" or "transition"): people adopt the new behaviours, processes or structures.
  3. Refreeze: stabilize the new state so it becomes the norm (some later writers say "freeze" or "refreezing").
- Related Lewin tool: **force field analysis**, which weighs *driving forces* against *restraining forces*; change happens when the driving forces outweigh the restraining ones or the restraining ones are reduced (general knowledge). Exam items may pair it with the unfreeze stage.
- Confusables: Lewin's leadership styles (autocratic, democratic, laissez-faire), already in the app as `lewin` (Domain 1). Questions must say "change model" or "unfreeze" so the two can't be mixed up. Kotter's three phase groups ("Defrosting the status quo", "Introducing new practices", "Making it stick") [S5] parallel Lewin's three stages. Bridges' three phases (Endings, Neutral Zone, New Beginnings) [S8] are also three steps but describe the inner transition, not the organizational change. Action research (also Lewin) belongs to `od`.
- Variants or contested points: the middle stage is named "change", "moving" or "transition" depending on the source. Historians (Cummings, Bridgman and Brown, *Human Relations*, 2016: general knowledge) argue that Lewin never set out a neat three-step model and that it was codified by later writers such as Edgar Schein. Don't write questions on that dispute.
- Unverified: all stage wording and the 1947 citation (general knowledge only). An author should use the plain names Unfreeze / Change / Refreeze and avoid testing the middle stage's alternate names.

### Bridges' transition model
- Origin: William Bridges, *Managing Transitions: Making the Most of Change* (1991; 4th ed. 2019 with Susan Bridges: general knowledge). The model is owned by William Bridges Associates [S8].
- Tie to the exam: named in 3.6.1 ("Bridges' transition model") [S1]. Not on the reading list [S3].
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: ordered (time order of a person's psychological transition)
- Key distinction, from the owner's page: **change** is "the external event or situation that takes place"; **transition** is "the inner psychological process that people go through" as they adjust to it. [S8] Exam items are likely to test this distinction ("Bridges focuses on transition, not change").
- Items, in the source's own terms [S8]:
  1. Endings: people recognize what they're losing or giving up and learn to handle those losses. Transition "starts with an ending". [S8]
  2. Neutral Zone: the old way is gone but the new one isn't fully working yet; the in-between period where psychological realignment happens. [S8]
  3. New Beginnings: new understandings, values and attitudes take hold, and energy goes in the new direction. [S8]
- Confusables: Lewin's three stages (organizational, not psychological); the Kübler-Ross change curve (also about emotional response, but five or more emotions, not three phases); Kotter.
- Variants or contested points: the book's chapter names are usually given as "Ending, Losing, and Letting Go", "The Neutral Zone" and "The New Beginning" (general knowledge); the owner's page says "Endings", "Neutral Zone", "New Beginnings" [S8]. Treat them as the same three phases. A common misattribution is calling Bridges a "change model": Bridges' own point is that it models transition, which is different from change. Whether the phases overlap: [S8] presents them in sequence (the neutral zone "comes after letting go"); many secondary sources say people can be in more than one phase at once (general knowledge, not seen).
- Unverified: the book's title, years and chapter wording (general knowledge only).

### Kübler-Ross change curve
- Origin: adapted from Elisabeth Kübler-Ross's stages of grief in *On Death and Dying* (1969). Her foundation says she "did not design a management model": she extended the stages to other losses and, "with her agreement", the workplace; business people who attended her workshops applied it to reorganizations, it was drawn as a curve in boardrooms by the 1980s, and her family began licensing it in 1995. [S9] "Kübler-Ross Change Curve" is a registered trademark (®) of the family partnership. [S9]
- Tie to the exam: named in 3.6.1 ("Kubler-Ross change curve") [S1]. Not on the reading list [S3].
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: set with a typical order. The owner calls them "seven areas of experience", not steps, and says they "can overlap, repeat, or be skipped" and "do not always occur in the same order". [S9] Don't write "what is the next stage" questions as if the order were fixed; a "typical sequence" question is defensible only with the owner's order below.
- Items, in the owner's terms and order [S9]:
  1. Shock: initial surprise or immobilization at the news of the change.
  2. Denial: refusing to believe or accept the change is happening.
  3. Frustration: anger and irritation as the change becomes real.
  4. Depression: low mood and energy, the bottom of the curve.
  5. Experiment: starting to try out the new situation.
  6. Decision: choosing to work within the new way.
  7. Integration: the change is accepted and absorbed into normal work.
  (One-line meanings are general paraphrase; the page was read only for names and order.)
- Groups: the page gives none. Many decks split the curve into a falling side (shock to depression) and a rising side (experiment to integration): general knowledge, not from [S9].
- Original grief stages (the source of the adaptation): *On Death and Dying* gave chapters to "denial and isolation, anger, bargaining, depression and acceptance". [S9] Usually shortened to the five stages Denial, Anger, Bargaining, Depression, Acceptance (DABDA).
- Confusables: the five stages of grief vs the seven-area change curve (different item lists; "anger" and "bargaining" are grief stages, "frustration", "experiment", "decision" and "integration" are change-curve terms); Bridges (three transition phases); the Satir change model (not named by ATD; avoid).
- Variants or contested points: the number of stages differs between sources (five grief stages; five-stage "change curve" versions that simply reuse DABDA; seven-area owner version; other decks show four zones). The common misattribution is to say Kübler-Ross designed the change curve for organizations; she wrote about dying patients, and the workplace use is an adaptation she agreed to [S9]. The original stages were also never meant as a fixed linear sequence [S9]. Which version ATD uses: unknown (the TDBoK text isn't available). Safest exam facts: it comes from the grief stages; it describes people's emotional reactions to change; the stages are not strictly linear.
- Unverified: whether ATD's TDBoK lists five or seven stages, and its exact stage names.

### Appreciative inquiry (AI)
- Origin: David Cooperrider, as a Case Western Reserve University graduate student working with his adviser Suresh Srivastva, early 1980s; first widely circulated as "Appreciative Inquiry in Organizational Life" in the mid-1980s. [S10] (The usual citation is Cooperrider & Srivastva, 1987, *Research in Organizational Change and Development*, vol. 1: general knowledge.)
- Tie to the exam: named in 3.6.1 ("appreciative inquiry") [S1]. Not on the reading list [S3]; it may also appear in *ATD's Organization Development Handbook* (3.3) but that wasn't checked.
- Suggested tag: named
- What it is: a strengths-based approach to change that inquires into what already works instead of diagnosing problems. Mohr calls it "a high-participation, full voice process targeted at organizational innovation" [S10] and states its premise that "organizations grow in the direction of what they repeatedly ask questions about". [S10]
- Kind: ordered (a cycle of phases)
- Items, 5-D version, in [S10]'s terms:
  1. Definition: set the focus and scope of the inquiry by choosing the topic and questions. [S10] "establishes the initial focus and scope of the inquiry"
  2. Discovery: interview many people to find best practices and the "life-giving forces" or "root causes of success". [S10]
  3. Dream: imagine the future the organization could have. [S10] "What is the world calling us to become?"
  4. Design: work out the high-leverage changes to systems, processes, roles, measures and structures needed for the dream. [S10]
  5. Delivery/Destiny: carry out, experiment with and refine the changes through "creativity, innovation, and iteration". [S10]
- 4-D version (general knowledge, not seen in a fetched source): Discovery ("what gives life", the best of what is), Dream ("what might be"), Design ("what should be"), Destiny ("what will be"; earlier called Delivery), with **affirmative topic choice** at the centre of the cycle rather than as a numbered phase (Cooperrider & Whitney).
- Confusables: problem-solving / deficit-based models such as action research (owned by `od`) and gap analysis (owned by `perf`), the classic "AI vs problem solving" contrast; design thinking (also has Define and ideate-like Dream/Design steps, see 3.8.3); SOAR (strengths, opportunities, aspirations, results), an AI-based alternative to SWOT (general knowledge; avoid unless sourced).
- Variants or contested points: 4-D (Discovery, Dream, Design, Destiny) vs 5-D (adds Define/Definition first). The fifth D's name varies ("Define", "Definition"), and the fourth phase is called Destiny or Delivery. Which version ATD uses: unknown. The safest questions use Discovery–Dream–Design–Destiny in that order and don't hinge on whether Define counts as a phase. AI's principles (constructionist, simultaneity, poetic, anticipatory, positive) are general knowledge only; [S10] names none.
- Unverified: the 4-D wording, affirmative topic choice, the five principles, the 1987 citation.

### Kübler-Ross five stages of grief (the source of the change curve)
- Origin: Elisabeth Kübler-Ross, *On Death and Dying*, 1969. [S9]
- Tie to the exam: the base of the change curve named in 3.6.1 [S1]; not named itself.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set with a typical order (not fixed; see change curve)
- Items, as the 1969 book's chapters are described by the foundation: "denial and isolation, anger, bargaining, depression and acceptance". [S9]
  1. Denial (and isolation)
  2. Anger
  3. Bargaining
  4. Depression
  5. Acceptance
- Confusables: the seven-area change curve (Shock, Denial, Frustration, Depression, Experiment, Decision, Integration) [S9]. Only Denial and Depression are in both lists.
- Variants or contested points: the stages were described for dying patients, not grieving survivors or employees; the extension to change is an adaptation (see the change curve entry). [S9]
- Unverified: nothing beyond the item meanings, which are self-explanatory.

### Lewin's force field analysis
- Origin: Kurt Lewin (field theory, 1940s; general knowledge, not fetched).
- Tie to the exam: part of "Lewin" in 3.6.1 [S1] by general knowledge only; ATD's link to it isn't shown.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set (two opposing groups of forces)
- Items (general knowledge):
  1. Driving forces: pressures pushing toward the change.
  2. Restraining forces: pressures resisting it.
  Change happens when the balance shifts: strengthen the driving forces or, usually more effectively, reduce the restraining ones. The current state is a "quasi-stationary equilibrium" between them (Lewin's term, general knowledge).
- Confusables: the three-stage change model (same author); stakeholder analysis (`biz`); SWOT (`strategy`).
- Unverified: all of it. Only use if an author needs a Lewin item beyond the three stages.

### ADKAR (Prosci)
- Origin: Jeff Hiatt, Prosci; book *ADKAR: A Model for Change in Business, Government and Our Community* (2006) (general knowledge, not fetched).
- Tie to the exam: not named in 3.6.1, not on the reading list [S3], and not mentioned in ATD's change capability blog post [S4]. It's covered because the ownership file lists it and it's a common exam-prep distractor.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: ordered (the sequence one individual moves through to change successfully)
- Items (general knowledge):
  1. Awareness: of the need for change.
  2. Desire: to take part in and support the change.
  3. Knowledge: of how to change.
  4. Ability: to put the change into practice (skills and behaviours).
  5. Reinforcement: to sustain the change.
- Confusables: Kotter (organizational steps, leader's view) vs ADKAR (individual outcomes); Kübler-Ross (emotions, not building blocks); Knowledge vs Ability (knowing how vs being able to do it; the classic swap).
- Variants or contested points: Prosci describes ADKAR as a model of *individual* change that rolls up into organizational change (general knowledge).
- Unverified: everything above (no Prosci page fetched). An author should keep ADKAR to letter meanings and the individual-change point, or leave it out.

### See other shards (3.6.1)
- See `od`: action research (also Lewin), systems thinking, open systems, chaos and complexity theory. A question that asks for a change model should not use these as distractors without a `kin` check, because action research is itself a change method.
- See `strategy`: SWOT; see `biz`: stakeholder analysis and organizational barriers (resistance to change is closely related to 3.2.8's barriers).

## 3.8.3: Techniques to promote innovation and creativity

### Design thinking (five stages)
- Origin: the five-stage version is usually credited to the Hasso Plattner Institute of Design at Stanford (the d.school); Tim Brown and IDEO popularized the term in business (*Harvard Business Review*, 2008). Both general knowledge; the ATD post credits nobody. [S11]
- Tie to the exam: named in 3.8.3 ("design thinking") [S1]; ATD describes it in [S11].
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: ordered (usual order of work), but iterative: ATD calls it "an iterative and collaborative methodology" and says "the process is nonlinear". [S11]
- Items, in ATD's terms [S11]:
  1. Empathize: learn what users think, feel, say and do, by listening and observing. [S11] "Empathize"
  2. Define: synthesize what you learned to state the real problem, asking "why" to find root causes. [S11] "Define"
  3. Ideate: generate many ideas without judging them yet. [S11] "Ideate"
  4. Prototype: build simple, low-cost mock-ups of the best ideas. [S11] "Prototype"
  5. Test: put prototypes in front of real users, watch their reactions, refine. [S11] "Test"
- Confusables: "ideation" is both the third design thinking stage and a separate named example in 3.8.3; questions mustn't offer both "design thinking" and "ideation" as options when either could be right. Appreciative inquiry's Define/Dream/Design (similar words, different model). Prototyping in SAM / instructional design (Domain 2 `sam`). The UK Design Council's Double Diamond (Discover, Define, Develop, Deliver) and IDEO's three spaces (inspiration, ideation, implementation) are other design-thinking versions (general knowledge, not seen).
- Variants or contested points: the number of stages varies (3, 4, 5, 6 or 7 across sources); the five-stage Empathize–Define–Ideate–Prototype–Test version is the one ATD's post uses [S11]. Empathy and user focus (human-centred design) are the distinguishing idea.
- Unverified: the d.school and IDEO credits.

### Brainstorming
- Origin: Alex F. Osborn, advertising executive (BBDO), *Your Creative Power* (1948) and *Applied Imagination* (1953): general knowledge. ATD [S12] does not credit Osborn.
- Tie to the exam: named in 3.8.3 ("brainstorming") [S1]. ATD lists it under both problem framing and idea generation and defines it as "generating ideas without judgment". [S12] [S11]
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: set (rules)
- Items, Osborn's four rules (general knowledge, not seen in a tier 1–3 source):
  1. Defer judgment: no criticism of ideas during the session.
  2. Go for quantity: the more ideas, the better the odds of a good one.
  3. Welcome wild or unusual ideas.
  4. Combine and improve: build on others' ideas ("piggybacking").
- Confusables: brainwriting (ideas written silently, then shared; a separate technique [S12]); nominal group technique (individual silent generation, round-robin sharing, then voting: general knowledge); ideation (the umbrella activity); the design thinking Ideate stage.
- Variants or contested points: research finds that individuals working alone (nominal groups) often produce more ideas than interactive brainstorming groups, partly because of production blocking (general knowledge; [S12] doesn't discuss it). Don't test this unless sourced.
- Unverified: Osborn as originator, the dates, the four rules' wording.

### Ideation and creativity techniques named by ATD
- Origin: ATD's summary of a study of creative problem-solving tools [S12].
- Tie to the exam: "ideation" is named in 3.8.3 [S1]; [S12] is ATD's own list of techniques.
- Suggested tag: named (ideation itself); extra (the individual techniques)
- Kind: set, in two groups
- Groups, in [S12]'s terms:
  - Problem framing: Brainstorming; Mind Mapping; Restating the Problem; the Five Ws and One H; Six Thinking Hats. [S12]
  - Idea generation: Analogical Thinking; Assumption Reversal; Brainstorming; Brainwriting; Checklisting/Forced Fitting; Morphological Matrix; SCAMPER. [S12]
  - Idea selection is "usually supported by convergent thinking". [S12]
- Confusables: brainstorming sits in both groups in [S12], so a "which group" question about brainstorming has two right answers. Divergent thinking (generating many options) vs convergent thinking (narrowing to the best): [S12] uses only "convergent"; the pair is general knowledge.
- Unverified: the meanings of the individual techniques (not fetched). General knowledge for the two most testable:
  - **SCAMPER** (Bob Eberle, 1971, from Osborn's idea-spurring checklist): Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify (also Magnify/Minify), Put to other uses, Eliminate, Reverse (or Rearrange). Letter meanings vary slightly between sources.
  - **Six Thinking Hats** (Edward de Bono, 1985): White = facts and information; Red = feelings and intuition; Black = caution and risks; Yellow = benefits and optimism; Green = creativity and new ideas; Blue = managing the thinking process.

### PESTLE / environmental scanning
- Origin: environmental scanning is usually traced to Francis Aguilar, *Scanning the Business Environment* (1967), whose ETPS (economic, technical, political, social) became PEST and then PESTLE (general knowledge, not fetched).
- Tie to the exam: not named in 3.8.1 and not on the reading list [S3]; it's the standard framework for "external factors". The ownership file assigns it here.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set
- Items (general knowledge):
  1. Political: government policy, stability, regulation priorities.
  2. Economic: growth, inflation, interest rates, labour costs and availability.
  3. Social (sociocultural): demographics, generational shifts, values, societal trends.
  4. Technological: new technology, automation, AI, rate of change.
  5. Legal: employment law, compliance, data protection.
  6. Environmental: climate, sustainability, natural resources.
