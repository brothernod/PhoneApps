## 3.6.1: Change management theories and models

### Kotter's 8-step process for leading change
- Origin: John P. Kotter, "Leading Change: Why Transformation Efforts Fail", *Harvard Business Review*, 1995, expanded into the book *Leading Change* (Harvard Business School Press, Boston, 1996; new-preface edition 2012). [S3] [S5] [S14] (Gap fill: the 1996 book, publisher and date are confirmed by [S14]; the 1995 article is still general knowledge.)
- Tie to the exam: named in 3.6.1 ("Kotter") [S1]; *Leading Change* (2012) is on ATD's reading list under 3.6 [S3].
- Suggested tag: reflist (named in the statement as well)
- Kind: ordered (the sequence in which a leader should drive a large change; [S5] numbers the steps and groups them in phases but does not say they must be strictly sequential)
- Items, in the 1996 book's own terms (gap fill: changed from [S5]'s wording to the book on ATD's reading list; chapter titles from [S14]), with [S5]'s Kotter International name after each:
  1. Establishing a sense of urgency: help others see why change is needed now. [S14] ([S5]: "Create a sense of urgency")
  2. Creating the guiding coalition: assemble a group with enough power and credibility to lead the change. [S14] ([S5]: "Build a guiding coalition")
  3. Developing a vision and strategy: say where the change is going and how. [S14] ([S5]: "Develop a vision and a strategy")
  4. Communicating the change vision: spread the vision so people buy in. [S14] ([S5]: "Communicate widely and enlist more supporters")
  5. Empowering employees for broad-based action: remove obstacles (structures, systems, people) that block the vision. [S14] ([S5]: "Enable action by removing barriers")
  6. Generating short-term wins: plan and celebrate visible early successes. [S14] ([S5]: "Generate short-term wins")
  7. Consolidating gains and producing more change: use credibility from wins to drive more change; don't declare victory early. [S14] ([S5]: "Consolidate gains and sustain acceleration")
  8. Anchoring new approaches in the culture: tie new behaviours to success so they stick. [S14] ([S5]: "Anchor new approaches in the culture to institute change")
  (One-line meanings are paraphrase from the first pass; [S14] gives titles only.)
- Groups (from [S5]): "Defrosting the status quo" → steps 1–4; "Introducing new practices" → steps 5–7; "Making it stick" → step 8. [S5]
- Confusables: Lewin's unfreeze–change–refreeze (Kotter's three groups map onto it: defrosting ≈ unfreeze, new practices ≈ change, making it stick ≈ refreeze); ADKAR (individual, not organizational, steps); Bridges (transition, not change); Kotter's eight *errors* (the mirror list in the same source, e.g. "Undercommunicating the vision by a factor of 10 (or 100 or even 1,000)" [S5]); Lewin's leadership styles (app id `lewin`, unrelated).
- Variants or contested points: the step names are worded differently in different Kotter publications, so questions should test the idea of each step, not one exact phrasing.
  - **1996 book wording, confirmed (gap fill)** from the book's table of contents [S14]. Part 2 is titled "The eight-stage process" (so the book calls them *stages*, not steps). Chapter titles, in order: 1 "Establishing a sense of urgency"; 2 "Creating the guiding coalition"; 3 "Developing a vision and strategy"; 4 "Communicating the change vision"; 5 "Empowering employees for broad-based action"; 6 "Generating short-term wins"; 7 "Consolidating gains and producing more change"; 8 "Anchoring new approaches in the culture". [S14] Note: the catalogue also lists a chapter "Developing the guiding coalition" between stages 2 and 3; that looks like a cataloguing duplicate (the book is commonly described as having eight stage chapters), so don't treat it as a ninth stage. Change from the first pass: stage 5 is "Empowering **employees** for broad-based action", not "Empowering broad-based action".
  - **Recommended item names for authors:** use the 1996 chapter wording above (the reading list's *Leading Change* is the 2012 reissue of this book [S3]), and keep [S5]'s present-tense names as acceptable paraphrases. The eight errors are presumably in Part 1's chapter "Transforming organizations: why firms fail" [S14] (title only; contents not seen).
  - *The Heart of Change* (Kotter & Cohen, 2002) style wording, as narrated by Mind Tools [S6]: "creating a sense of urgency", "putting together a guiding team", "developing a change vision and strategy", "communicating the vision", "empowering others to act", "producing short-term wins", "don't let up", "making change stick". [S6]
  - Current Kotter Inc wording (kotterinc.com, 2026) [S7]: 1 "Create A Sense of Urgency", 2 "Build A Guiding Coalition", 3 "Form A Strategic Vision", 4 "Enlist A Volunteer Army", 5 "Enable Action By Removing Barriers", 6 "Generate Short-Term Wins", 7 "Sustain Acceleration", 8 "Institute Change". The firm says it "evolved the linear 8 Step" process from *Leading Change* into the "8 Accelerators" of *Accelerate* (2014) and the firm's book *CHANGE* (2021). [S7] So "volunteer army", "sustain acceleration" and "institute change" belong to the 2014-on version, not the 1996 book. The idea that the accelerators run concurrently and through a "dual operating system" is general knowledge about *Accelerate*; [S7] says only "linear" (old) vs "dynamic" (new).
  - Which version ATD uses: the reading list names *Leading Change* (2012 edition of the 1996 book) [S3], so the 1996 eight-stage process is the safest basis. Sequence matters in that version (Kotter warns skipping steps creates only an illusion of speed: general knowledge, not seen).
- Gap fill, ATD and deck wording: ATD's blog [S20] calls it "Kotter's eight-step model" and opens with "creating a sense of urgency", then "creating a coalition". A TDBoK-keyed deck [S21] gives sense of urgency, forming a powerful coalition, creating a vision for change, communicating the vision, removing obstacles, creating short-term wins, building on the change, anchoring the changes (tier 4; this phrasing matches neither the 1996 book nor Kotter Inc's current page, so the TDBoK may paraphrase). The deck also mentions a "Kotter's change bottle" card; not understood, don't use.
- Unverified: the "skipping steps" warning (general knowledge only); [S5]'s page date. (Gap fill: the 1996 stage wording is now confirmed from [S14].) The often-quoted "70% of change efforts fail" figure was not found in any source here: don't use it.

### Lewin's change model (unfreeze, change, refreeze)
- Origin: Kurt Lewin, "Frontiers in Group Dynamics", *Human Relations* 1(1), 5–41, 1947 (gap fill: confirmed by Burnes, who quotes it with page numbers [S13]). Lewin called it a "Three Step Procedure" (Lewin 1947: 10); "Lewin's Three Step Model" is the name it got after his death. [S13]
- Tie to the exam: 3.6.1 names "Lewin" with no model specified [S1]. In a change management statement this means the change model, not Lewin's leadership styles. ATD's reading list has no Lewin title [S3].
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: ordered (the three stages in time order)
- Lewin's own words (1947: 35, quoted in [S13]): a successful change has "three aspects": "unfreezing (if necessary) the present level L1", "moving to the new level L2", and "freezing group life on the new level". So Lewin's own stage names are **unfreezing, moving, freezing**. [S13] Burnes notes "It is now usual for the Freezing step to be referred to as Refreezing" [S13]; he doesn't say who introduced "refreezing".
- Items (the usual modern labels, each matched to Lewin's own term [S13]):
  1. Unfreeze (Lewin: "unfreezing (if necessary) the present level"): create readiness by making the need for change felt and loosening the current equilibrium. [S13]
  2. Change (Lewin: "moving to the new level"): people adopt the new behaviours, processes or structures. [S13]
  3. Refreeze (Lewin: "freezing group life on the new level"): stabilize the new state so it becomes the norm. [S13]
  (One-line meanings are paraphrase; [S13] was read for the quote and names only.)
- Related Lewin tool: **force field analysis**, which weighs *driving forces* against *restraining forces*; change happens when the driving forces outweigh the restraining ones or the restraining ones are reduced (general knowledge). Exam items may pair it with the unfreeze stage.
- Confusables: Lewin's leadership styles (autocratic, democratic, laissez-faire), already in the app as `lewin` (Domain 1). Questions must say "change model" or "unfreeze" so the two can't be mixed up. Kotter's three phase groups ("Defrosting the status quo", "Introducing new practices", "Making it stick") [S5] parallel Lewin's three stages. Bridges' three phases (Endings, Neutral Zone, New Beginnings) [S8] are also three steps but describe the inner transition, not the organizational change. Action research (also Lewin) belongs to `od`.
- Variants or contested points: the middle stage is named "change", "moving" (Lewin's own word [S13]) or "transition" depending on the source; the last is "freezing" (Lewin) or "refreezing" (now usual [S13]). Historians (Cummings, Bridgman and Brown, *Human Relations*, 2016: general knowledge) argue that Lewin never set out a neat three-step model; Burnes [S13] rejects that claim and argues the labels were new names for ideas Lewin had developed since the 1920s, used in only three of his final publications. Don't write questions on that dispute.
- ATD's own blog [S20] uses "unfreeze the current behavior", "make the change", "refreeze the new behavior", and suggests Lewin's model for organizations that need a less sophisticated approach. A TDBoK-keyed deck [S21] gives "unfreezing, changing, refreezing" and says the chapter also covers force field analysis (tier 4).
- Unverified: which labels the TDBoK uses (deck says unfreezing/changing/refreezing [S21]). Authors should use Unfreeze / Change / Refreeze, may give "moving" and "freezing" as Lewin's original terms (sourced [S13]), and should not offer "transition" as a distractor for the middle stage (it is a real variant and also Bridges' word).

### Bridges' transition model
- Origin: William Bridges, *Managing Transitions: Making the Most of Change* (1991; 4th ed. 2019 with Susan Bridges: general knowledge). The model is owned by William Bridges Associates [S8].
- Tie to the exam: named in 3.6.1 ("Bridges' transition model") [S1]. Not on the reading list [S3].
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: ordered (time order of a person's psychological transition)
- Key distinction, from the owner's page: **change** is "the external event or situation that takes place"; **transition** is "the inner psychological process that people go through" as they adjust to it. [S8] Exam items are likely to test this distinction ("Bridges focuses on transition, not change").
- Items, in the source's own terms [S8]:
  1. Endings: people recognize what they're losing or giving up and learn to handle those losses. Transition "starts with an ending". [S8]
  2. Neutral Zone: the old way is gone but the new one isn't fully working yet; the in-between period where psychological realignment happens. [S8]
  3. New Beginnings: new understandings, values and attitudes take hold, and energy goes in the new direction. [S8]
- Confusables: Lewin's three stages (organizational, not psychological); the Kübler-Ross change curve (also about emotional response, but five or more emotions, not three phases); Kotter.
- Variants or contested points: the book's chapter names are usually given as "Ending, Losing, and Letting Go", "The Neutral Zone" and "The New Beginning" (general knowledge); the owner's page says "Endings", "Neutral Zone", "New Beginnings" [S8]. Treat them as the same three phases. A common misattribution is calling Bridges a "change model": Bridges' own point is that it models transition, which is different from change. Whether the phases overlap: [S8] presents them in sequence (the neutral zone "comes after letting go"); many secondary sources say people can be in more than one phase at once (general knowledge, not seen).
- Gap fill: a search for the book's contents returned only summary sites (Mind Tools, Shortform, Blinkist), so the book's chapter wording is still unseen; the owner's page [S8] remains the "own words" source. A TDBoK-keyed deck [S21] gives "ending, neutral zone, new beginnings", matching [S8] (tier 4). ATD's blog [S20] calls it Bridges' "transitions model" and describes a loss phase, an "awkward transition" and a new routine, without formal names.
- Unverified: the book's title, years and chapter wording (general knowledge only). Use the owner's names: Endings, Neutral Zone, New Beginnings [S8].

### Kübler-Ross change curve
- Origin: adapted from Elisabeth Kübler-Ross's stages of grief in *On Death and Dying* (1969). Her foundation says she "did not design a management model": she extended the stages to other losses and, "with her agreement", the workplace; business people who attended her workshops applied it to reorganizations, it was drawn as a curve in boardrooms by the 1980s, and her family began licensing it in 1995. [S9] "Kübler-Ross Change Curve" is a registered trademark (®) of the family partnership. [S9]
- Tie to the exam: named in 3.6.1 ("Kubler-Ross change curve") [S1]. Not on the reading list [S3].
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: set with a typical order. The owner calls them "seven areas of experience", not steps, and says they "can overlap, repeat, or be skipped" and "do not always occur in the same order". [S9] Don't write "what is the next stage" questions as if the order were fixed; a "typical sequence" question is defensible only with the owner's order below.
- Items, in the owner's terms and order [S9]:
  1. Shock: initial surprise or immobilization at the news of the change.
  2. Denial: refusing to believe or accept the change is happening.
  3. Frustration: anger and irritation as the change becomes real.
  4. Depression: low mood and energy, the bottom of the curve.
  5. Experiment: starting to try out the new situation.
  6. Decision: choosing to work within the new way.
  7. Integration: the change is accepted and absorbed into normal work.
  (One-line meanings are general paraphrase; the page was read only for names and order.)
- Groups: the page gives none. Many decks split the curve into a falling side (shock to depression) and a rising side (experiment to integration): general knowledge, not from [S9].
- Original grief stages (the source of the adaptation): *On Death and Dying* gave chapters to "denial and isolation, anger, bargaining, depression and acceptance". [S9] Usually shortened to the five stages Denial, Anger, Bargaining, Depression, Acceptance (DABDA).
- Confusables: the five stages of grief vs the seven-area change curve (different item lists; "anger" and "bargaining" are grief stages, "frustration", "experiment", "decision" and "integration" are change-curve terms); Bridges (three transition phases); the Satir change model (not named by ATD; avoid).
- Variants or contested points: the number of stages differs between sources (five grief stages; five-stage "change curve" versions that simply reuse DABDA; seven-area owner version; other decks show four zones). The common misattribution is to say Kübler-Ross designed the change curve for organizations; she wrote about dying patients, and the workplace use is an adaptation she agreed to [S9]. The original stages were also never meant as a fixed linear sequence [S9]. Which version ATD uses: unknown (the TDBoK text isn't available). Safest exam facts: it comes from the grief stages; it describes people's emotional reactions to change; the stages are not strictly linear.
- Gap fill: a TDBoK-keyed deck [S21] gives the five grief stages (denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance) for this model and calls it a "change response cycle" (tier 4). So the TDBoK probably uses the five-stage version, not the owner's seven areas. Authors: either avoid stage-list questions, or present the five stages as "the stages of grief that the change curve is based on", which is true under both versions [S9].
- Unverified: the TDBoK's own wording (deck evidence only) and the "change response cycle" label.

### Appreciative inquiry (AI)
- Origin: David Cooperrider, as a Case Western Reserve University graduate student working with his adviser Suresh Srivastva, early 1980s; first widely circulated as "Appreciative Inquiry in Organizational Life" in the mid-1980s. [S10] (The usual citation is Cooperrider & Srivastva, 1987, *Research in Organizational Change and Development*, vol. 1: general knowledge.)
- Tie to the exam: named in 3.6.1 ("appreciative inquiry") [S1]. Not on the reading list [S3]; it may also appear in *ATD's Organization Development Handbook* (3.3) but that wasn't checked.
- Suggested tag: named
- What it is: a strengths-based approach to change that inquires into what already works instead of diagnosing problems. Mohr calls it "a high-participation, full voice process targeted at organizational innovation" [S10] and states its premise that "organizations grow in the direction of what they repeatedly ask questions about". [S10]
- Kind: ordered (a cycle of phases)
- Items, 5-D version, in [S10]'s terms:
  1. Definition: set the focus and scope of the inquiry by choosing the topic and questions. [S10] "establishes the initial focus and scope of the inquiry"
  2. Discovery: interview many people to find best practices and the "life-giving forces" or "root causes of success". [S10]
  3. Dream: imagine the future the organization could have. [S10] "What is the world calling us to become?"
  4. Design: work out the high-leverage changes to systems, processes, roles, measures and structures needed for the dream. [S10]
  5. Delivery/Destiny: carry out, experiment with and refine the changes through "creativity, innovation, and iteration". [S10]
- **4-D version (gap fill, partly confirmed)**: Cooperrider & Whitney's own book [S15] calls AI "a new model of change management" and names the four phases through its chapter headings: "The First D, Discovery", "From Discovery to Dream", "Design for Organizing into the Future", "Realizing Destiny". [S15] So the originators' four Ds, in order, are **Discovery, Dream, Design, Destiny**. [S15] The book also has a section "The Positive Core". [S15] Still general knowledge only (not in [S15]'s excerpt or [S16]): the tag lines Discovery "what gives life", Dream "what might be", Design "what should be", Destiny "what will be", and **affirmative topic choice** at the centre of the cycle. Use the four names and their order; don't test the tag lines or the centre. A TDBoK-keyed deck [S21] calls it the "4D model" and gives discovery "what is", dream "what might be", design "what should be", destiny "what will be" (tier 4: suggests the TDBoK uses the 4-D version, and that its Discovery tag line may be "what is" rather than "what gives life").
- Principles: see the next entry, "Appreciative inquiry's five classic principles".
- Confusables: problem-solving / deficit-based models such as action research (owned by `od`) and gap analysis (owned by `perf`), the classic "AI vs problem solving" contrast; design thinking (also has Define and ideate-like Dream/Design steps, see 3.8.3); SOAR (strengths, opportunities, aspirations, results), an AI-based alternative to SWOT (general knowledge; avoid unless sourced).
- Variants or contested points: 4-D (Discovery, Dream, Design, Destiny: the originators' book [S15]) vs 5-D (adds Define/Definition first [S10]; the Cooperrider Center also has a "5-D Cycle" page [S16], not read). The fifth D's name varies ("Define", "Definition"), and the fourth phase is called Destiny or Delivery. Which version ATD uses: probably 4-D (deck evidence only [S21]). The safest questions use Discovery–Dream–Design–Destiny in that order and don't hinge on whether Define counts as a phase.

### Appreciative inquiry's five classic principles (added in gap fill)
- Origin: Cooperrider & Whitney, *Appreciative Inquiry: A Positive Revolution in Change* (2005), which lists the five as chapter sections [S15]; definitions from the Cooperrider Center's AI Commons [S16].
- Tie to the exam: part of "appreciative inquiry", named in 3.6.1 [S1]. Not seen in an ATD source.
- Suggested tag: extra (named via AI, but the principles themselves aren't shown in an ATD source)
- Kind: set ([S15] lists them in this order; the order is not a sequence)
- Items, in the originators' terms [S15] [S16]:
  1. Constructionist Principle: reality is "socially created through language and conversations". [S16]
  2. Simultaneity Principle: inquiry and change happen together; "The moment we ask a question, we begin to create a change." [S16]
  3. Poetic Principle: the topics we choose to inquire into shape what we find; "What we choose to study makes a difference." [S16]
  4. Anticipatory Principle: "Human systems move in the direction of their images of the future." [S16]
  5. Positive Principle: change is generated through "positive questions that amplify the positive core". [S16]
- Confusables: Anticipatory (images of the future) vs Poetic (choice of topic) vs Simultaneity (asking is itself change). Mohr's premise that organizations grow toward what they ask about [S10] is close to both the Poetic and Anticipatory principles, so don't key a question on that sentence alone. Senge's five disciplines (app id `senge`, also five items, also "shared vision"-like language).
- Variants or contested points: later writers add further "emergent" principles (general knowledge); the five here are the "classic" set [S16].
- Unverified: nothing in the item list; the later emergent principles were not checked.

### Lewin's force field analysis
- Origin: Kurt Lewin's field theory. The "quasi-stationary equilibrium of driving and restraining forces" goes back to Lewin's *Principles of Topological Psychology* (1936), and the forces are discussed in his 1943 papers (e.g. "Forces behind food habits"), as cited by Burnes [S13]. (Gap fill: confirmed. The name "force field analysis" for the tool is general knowledge; [S13] was read only for the terms below.)
- Tie to the exam: part of "Lewin" in 3.6.1 [S1] by general knowledge only; ATD's link to it isn't shown. Burnes treats field theory as one of the theories and tools that underpin the Three Step model [S13], so it belongs with the change model.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set (two opposing groups of forces)
- Items, in Lewin's terms (gap fill, confirmed via [S13]):
  1. Driving forces: pressures pushing toward the change. [S13] "driving and restraining forces"
  2. Restraining forces: pressures resisting it. [S13]
  The current state is a "quasi-stationary equilibrium" in which the forces are "in balance" [S13]. To change it, the balance must be upset [S13], either "by adding forces in the desired direction or by diminishing opposing forces"; Lewin adds that "as a rule, the second method will be preferable" (Lewin 1947, "Frontiers in Group Dynamics", p. 26, quoted in [S13]).
- Confusables: the three-stage change model (same author); stakeholder analysis (`biz`); SWOT (`strategy`).
- Exam-ready facts (sourced [S13]): the two kinds of force are *driving* and *restraining*; the status quo is a *quasi-stationary equilibrium*; Lewin preferred *reducing restraining forces* over adding driving forces. The link to the unfreeze stage (reducing restraining forces is how one unfreezes) is a common teaching point but our inference.
- Unverified: whether ATD ties force field analysis to 3.6.1; the usual worksheet steps (list forces, score them, plan actions), which are general knowledge only. (Gap fill: resolved the terms and the preferred method [S13].)

## 3.8.3: Techniques to promote innovation and creativity

### Design thinking (five stages)
- Origin: the five-mode version is the Hasso Plattner Institute of Design at Stanford's (the d.school's): its *Bootcamp Bootleg* lists the modes Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test, in that order. [S19] (Gap fill: confirmed.) The d.school calls them "modes"; ATD's post calls them stages or steps [S11]. Tim Brown and IDEO popularized the term in business (*Harvard Business Review*, 2008: general knowledge). The ATD post credits nobody. [S11]
- Tie to the exam: named in 3.8.3 ("design thinking") [S1]; ATD describes it in [S11].
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: ordered (usual order of work), but iterative: ATD calls it "an iterative and collaborative methodology" and says "the process is nonlinear". [S11]
- Items, in ATD's terms [S11]:
  1. Empathize: learn what users think, feel, say and do, by listening and observing. [S11] "Empathize"
  2. Define: synthesize what you learned to state the real problem, asking "why" to find root causes. [S11] "Define"
  3. Ideate: generate many ideas without judging them yet. [S11] "Ideate"
  4. Prototype: build simple, low-cost mock-ups of the best ideas. [S11] "Prototype"
  5. Test: put prototypes in front of real users, watch their reactions, refine. [S11] "Test"
- Confusables: "ideation" is both the third design thinking stage and a separate named example in 3.8.3; questions mustn't offer both "design thinking" and "ideation" as options when either could be right. Appreciative inquiry's Define/Dream/Design (similar words, different model). Prototyping in SAM / instructional design (Domain 2 `sam`). The UK Design Council's Double Diamond (Discover, Define, Develop, Deliver) and IDEO's three spaces (inspiration, ideation, implementation) are other design-thinking versions (general knowledge, not seen).
- Variants or contested points: the number of stages varies (3, 4, 5, 6 or 7 across sources); the five-stage Empathize–Define–Ideate–Prototype–Test version is the one ATD's post uses [S11]. Empathy and user focus (human-centred design) are the distinguishing idea.
- d.school's own core idea per mode [S19] (read in Japanese translation; paraphrased): Empathize is the foundation of human-centred design; Define turns the needs and insights found while empathizing into a specific, meaningful challenge; Ideate is the mode focused on generating ideas; Prototype gets ideas out of your head into the physical world; Test is the chance to refine solutions, and it informs the next prototype iteration (sometimes sending you back to an earlier mode). [S19]
- Unverified: the IDEO / Tim Brown credit (general knowledge). (Gap fill: d.school origin and mode order confirmed [S19].)

### Brainstorming
- Origin: Alex F. Osborn, a partner at the advertising agency BBDO, who "first organized such group thinking in our company" in 1939 and introduced brainstorming to the public in his best-seller *Your Creative Power* (1948). [S18] Osborn's definition: "using the brain to storm a creative problem". [S18] (*Applied Imagination*, 1953, is the usual citation for the full method: general knowledge, not in [S18].) ATD [S12] does not credit Osborn.
- Tie to the exam: named in 3.8.3 ("brainstorming") [S1]. ATD lists it under both problem framing and idea generation and defines it as "generating ideas without judgment". [S12] [S11]
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: set (rules)
- Items, Osborn's four rules, in Osborn's wording as quoted by [S18] (gap fill):
  1. Criticism ruled out: "Judicial judgment is ruled out"; criticism of ideas is "withheld until the next day". [S18] Modern shorthand: **defer judgment**.
  2. "Wildness" is welcomed: unusual, far-out ideas are encouraged. [S18] (Often printed as "Freewheeling is welcomed": general knowledge.) Modern shorthand: **welcome wild ideas**.
  3. Quantity is wanted: aim for as many ideas as possible. [S18] Modern shorthand: **go for quantity**.
  4. Combination and improvement are sought: build on and join others' ideas (the "piggybacking" label is general knowledge). [S18] Modern shorthand: **combine and improve**.
  (Meanings are paraphrase; [S18] gives the rule names.)
  (Kind: set; [S18] lists them in this order, but the order isn't a sequence.)
- d.school brainstorming rules (gap fill, a later variant): the d.school *Bootcamp Bootleg* [S19] gives eight brainstorming rules. Read only in Japanese translation; our back-translations: avoid evaluation/judgment; rough (wild) ideas welcome; quantity over quality; build on others' ideas; stay on topic; one idea at a time; be visual; think in headlines. [S19] Four of these restate Osborn's four rules; the other four are d.school/IDEO additions. The English wording (commonly "Defer judgment", "Encourage wild ideas", "Go for volume", "Build on the ideas of others", "Stay focused on topic", "One conversation at a time", "Be visual", "Headline!") is general knowledge, not seen. Authors: key questions on Osborn's four only.
- Confusables: brainwriting (ideas written silently, then shared; a separate technique [S12]); nominal group technique (individual silent generation, round-robin sharing, then voting: general knowledge); ideation (the umbrella activity); the design thinking Ideate stage.
- Variants or contested points: research finds that individuals working alone (nominal groups) often produce more ideas than interactive brainstorming groups, partly because of production blocking (general knowledge; [S12] doesn't discuss it). Don't test this unless sourced.
- Unverified: the *Applied Imagination* (1953) citation and the "Freewheeling" wording (general knowledge). [S18] is a college profile, not Osborn's book itself, so its wording is tier "other" but quotes Osborn directly. (Gap fill: originator, 1939 BBDO start, 1948 book and the four rules now sourced [S18].)

### Ideation and creativity techniques named by ATD

- Tie to the exam: "ideation" is named in 3.8.3 [S1]; [S12] is ATD's own list of techniques.
