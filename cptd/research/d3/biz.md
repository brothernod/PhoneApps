## 3.1.7 (3.1.A): Business cases

No tier 1–3 source read for this shard gives a named list of business-case components. ATD's reading list has no business-case title [S3]; the one ATD article found [S10] gives no component list. What follows is the deck-level picture [S9] plus general business practice, tagged accordingly.

### Business case: definition and components (deck-level and general practice)
- Origin: general business practice; deck wording in [S9]. No single author.
- Tie to the exam: 3.1.7 names business cases and the three data types they use (economic, financial, organizational).
- Suggested tag: extra (named concept; component list unverified in tiers 1–3)
- Kind: set, with a conventional order (executive summary first)
- Definition: a document presenting the rationale and justification for a project, presented to senior management [S9] (deck card 1; distractors were general ledger, action plan, annual plan).
- Statement's three data types (handbook wording [S1]): economic, financial, organizational. Examples (general knowledge, not from an ATD source): economic = industry or labor-market conditions, competitor moves; financial = costs, budgets, projected benefits, ROI/BCR; organizational = strategy, goals, KPIs, headcount, turnover, engagement.
- Items as the deck uses them (deck-only unless marked):
  1. Executive summary: comes first [S9] (card 10: "should begin with an executive summary"; cost-benefit and risk evaluation come later).
  2. Need or opportunity, aligned to strategic priorities: first check alignment with organizational strategic priorities [S9] (card 4); then identify and compare different solutions [S9] (card 21).
  3. Business and operational impact: what the deck says rejected cases lacked; a case that focused on the implementation plan and timeline should have focused on business and operational impacts [S9] (cards 5, 25).
  4. Cost-benefit analysis: presented to justify the investment (card 7); stakeholders look for contribution to organizational value and a good return on investment (cards 9, 24) [S9].
  5. Risk evaluation: later in the document than the summary [S9] (card 10).
  6. Approximate budget: a business case may include only an approximate budget; exact numbers are not expected [S9] (card 23).
- Common in general business-case templates but not seen in any source read here: options analysis including "do nothing", assumptions, implementation timeline, recommendation, success metrics. Use only as plausible distractor-free background.
- Confusables: project charter (`charter`, Domain 1; it authorizes a project already approved, while a business case argues for approval); value proposition (`value-prop`, Domain 1); action plan; annual training plan; budget (a business case includes cost estimates but is not the budget).
- Variants or contested points: none found; the deck's sequencing ("first create a business case for stakeholders" before talking to employees and IT, card 3) is a deck judgment, not a sourced rule.
- Unverified: the whole component list (tier 4 only). The TDBoK chapter 3.1 text was not available.


### Financial metrics for investment decisions (ROI, BCR, payback period, NPV, IRR, break-even)
- Origin: Phillips and Phillips, *ROI Basics*, ATD Press 2019, ch. 1, Table 1-1 and text [S11]. ROI and BCR formulas are already in the app (`roi-formulas`, `phillips-levels`): reference them, don't redo them. This entry adds the other metrics and how Phillips (ATD Press) positions them.
- Tie to the exam: 3.1.7 ("financial ... data" in a business case); 3.1.6. Phillips's ROI books are ATD Press titles; whether *ROI Basics* itself is on the 2025 reading list was not checked (Phillips titles sit under 2.8).
- Suggested tag: extra (named by the shard brief; tier 3 source)
- Kind: set
- Items, in the source's terms:
  1. Benefit-cost ratio (BCR): program benefits ÷ program costs; uses **gross** benefits. [S11] "BCR, compares gross benefits to costs." See `roi-formulas`.
  2. Return on investment (ROI %): net program benefits ÷ program costs × 100; uses **net** benefits. [S11] A BCR of 3:1 means an ROI of 200%, not 300% ("is not possible"). See `roi-formulas`.
  3. Payback period (PP): the length of time to recover an investment; PP = program costs ÷ program benefits (Table 1-1 form). Using annual benefits gives years, and × 12 gives months (general Phillips usage; not seen in the excerpt). [S11] "Measures the length of time to recover an investment." Phillips notes PP ignores the time value of money and future benefits, and that it indicates the break-even point.
  4. Break-even: the point where benefits equal costs, i.e. BCR 1:1, which is ROI 0%. [S11] "indicates the break-even point, or a BCR of 1:1"
  5. Net present value (NPV): the difference between the present value of cash inflows and the present value of cash outflows. [S11] "the difference between the present value of cash inflows and the present value of cash outflows"
  6. Internal rate of return (IRR): the discount rate that makes the NPV of all of a project's cash flows equal zero; used in capital budgeting. [S11] "Used in capital budgeting."
- Groups: Phillips separates metrics suited to TD (ROI, BCR, PP) from capital-budgeting metrics (NPV, IRR), saying not all metrics suit "non-capital expenditures such as talent development" [S11]. So for ATD purposes, NPV and IRR are terms to recognize, while ROI/BCR/PP are what TD reports.
- Related principles [S11]: BCR is the output of benefit-cost analysis, grounded in welfare economics; Jack Phillips added cost-benefit analysis to the "four-step" evaluation concept (the excerpt's term for Kirkpatrick's levels) to create the ROI model; ROI is "the ultimate measure" but must be reported with other performance measures; Guiding Principle 10: fully load all costs (needs assessment when conducted, design, delivery, evaluation).
- Confusables: ROI vs BCR (gross vs net benefits; % vs ratio: the classic trap); payback period vs break-even (PP is the time to reach break-even); NPV vs IRR (an amount vs a rate); cost-benefit analysis (the method) vs BCR (its output); `roi-isolate`, `roi-convert` (other steps of the Phillips process).
- Variants or contested points: **conventions differ outside ATD.** (a) In corporate finance, ROI is often quoted as a ratio or as (gain − cost) ÷ cost without "× 100"; Phillips/ATD always expresses ROI as a percentage of net benefits over fully loaded costs. (b) Some finance texts compute payback from cumulative (or discounted) cash flows year by year; Phillips uses the simple costs ÷ annual benefits form. (c) "Break-even" in accounting is often a unit volume (fixed costs ÷ contribution margin per unit); in Phillips it is BCR 1:1 / ROI 0%. (d) NPV sign convention: positive NPV means the investment earns more than the discount rate; Phillips gives no decision rule. Use Phillips's version on the exam.
- Unverified: the other rows of Table 1-1 (only PP, NPV and IRR were read beyond ROI and BCR); the edition number of *ROI Basics*.

### Capital vs operating expenses, and cost categories (general knowledge only)
- Origin: general accounting practice; no ATD or original-author source was read for this shard. The budgeting titles' pages [S4][S5][S6] and the deck [S9] do not mention these terms.
- Tie to the exam: 3.1.6 and 3.1.7 (financial data); named in the shard's ownership brief.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: pairs of contrasting categories (sets)
- Items (general knowledge; mark every one as unverified):
  1. Capital expenditure (capex): spending on long-lived assets (e.g. building a learning center, buying hardware, sometimes developing an LMS), recorded as an asset and depreciated or amortized over its useful life.
  2. Operating expenditure (opex): day-to-day running costs (salaries, vendor fees, licenses or subscriptions, travel, supplies), expensed in the period incurred. SaaS learning platforms are usually opex.
  3. Fixed vs variable costs: fixed costs don't change with volume (staff salaries, facility lease, platform license); variable costs rise with number of learners or sessions (materials, per-seat fees, travel).
  4. Direct vs indirect costs: direct costs are traceable to one program (facilitator fee, materials); indirect costs (overhead) are shared across programs (TD department administration, facilities, IT support) and are allocated.
  5. One-time vs recurring: development costs vs ongoing delivery and maintenance costs.
- Confusables: Phillips's fully loaded cost categories (needs assessment when conducted, design, delivery, evaluation [S11]; overhead appears in his longer lists, general knowledge only); capex vs capital budgeting (the NPV/IRR decision process).
- Unverified: all of the above in tiers 1–3. Authors should keep any question on these to standard definitions and avoid "according to ATD".

## 3.2 (all four statements): the consulting process

These serve the whole 3.2 capability ("Consulting & Business Partnering"). The closest single statement is noted on each.

### Block's five phases of consulting (Flawless Consulting)
- Origin: Peter Block, *Flawless Consulting*, 1st ed. 1981; 2nd ed. 1999/2000; 3rd ed. 2011; 4th ed. 2023 (Wiley). [S7][S8]
- Tie to the exam: 3.2 overall; phase 3 is closest to 3.2.5 (formulating recommendations and gaining agreement to act), phase 1 to 3.2.1 (establishing the relationship). Not on ATD's reading list [S3] and not shown in TDBoK text. Owner of this model per OWNERSHIP.md: `biz`.
- Suggested tag: extra (named by the shard brief, not by the handbook statement; not on the reading list)
- Kind: ordered (the sequence of a consulting engagement, first to last)
- Items, in the 4th edition's terms [S7], with the 2nd edition's terms where they differ [S8]:
  1. Entry and Contracting: initial contact with the client about the project, and agreeing on what each side wants and how they will work together. [S7] "Entry and Contracting"
  2. Discovery (2nd ed.: "Discovery and Dialogue"): the consultant's own sense of the problem, gathering data and seeing the situation in the client's context. [S7] "Discovery"; [S8] "Phase 2. Discovery and Dialogue"
  3. Analysis and the Decision to Act (2nd ed.: "Feedback and the Decision to Act"): reducing the data to a manageable picture, feeding it back to the client, and the client choosing what to do. [S7] "Analysis and the Decision to Act"; [S8] "Phase 3. Feedback and the Decision to Act"
  4. Engagement and Implementation: carrying out the planning from phase 3; in Block's later editions this stresses engaging the people affected rather than just installing a program. [S7][S8] "Engagement and Implementation"
  5. Extension, Recycle, or Termination: evaluating the engagement and deciding whether to extend it, cycle back to an earlier phase, or end it. [S7][S8] "Extension, Recycle, or Termination"
- Confusables: the action research cycle and ATD's OD process (owner `od`); the HPI/ATD performance improvement model and Robinson's performance consulting process (owner `perf`); ADDIE (`addie`); Domain 1 `pm-phases` (initiating … closing). The word "contracting" here means agreeing expectations with the client, not a legal or procurement contract.
- Variants or contested points: **phase names change by edition.** 2nd ed. (1999/2000): "Discovery and Dialogue", "Feedback and the Decision to Act" [S8]. 4th ed. (2023): "Discovery", "Analysis and the Decision to Act" [S7]. The 2nd edition's chapter titles still use older words ("Getting the Data", "From Diagnosis to Discovery", "Preparing for Feedback") [S8], and the 1st edition (1981) is widely cited with "Data Collection and Diagnosis" as phase 2 (not verified here). Study guides mix these. Phases 1, 4 and 5 are the same in both editions read. Safest exam framing: use the stable names (phase 1, 4, 5) and accept either edition's wording for 2 and 3.
- Unverified: the one-line meanings above are paraphrases from general knowledge of the book; the excerpt read [S7] confirmed the phase names and their order, not their definitions. 1st-edition wording not checked.

### Block's three consultant roles
- Origin: Block, *Flawless Consulting*, section "Roles Consultants Choose" (2nd ed. section 2.5) [S8]; same three roles in the 4th ed. [S7]. The 4th-edition excerpt discusses them in connection with Ed Schein (see Variants).
- Tie to the exam: 3.2.1 (choosing how to work with a client is the start of the partnership) and 3.2.2. Not on the reading list.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set (often taught as a continuum of shared responsibility, pair-of-hands and expert at the ends and collaborative in between in terms of who decides, but Block lists them as three choices)
- Items, in the source's terms:
  1. Expert Role: the client hands the problem to the consultant, who diagnoses and decides what to do; the client is fairly passive. [S7] "Expert Role"
  2. Pair-of-Hands Role: the client keeps control and decides; the consultant applies specialized knowledge to carry out what the client has already chosen. [S7] "Pair-of-Hands Role"
  3. Collaborative Role: consultant and client work as partners, share data, decisions and responsibility, so problems stay solved and the client learns to solve them. [S7] "Collaborative Role"
- Block's preferred role: collaborative (general knowledge; the excerpt was cut off inside the Collaborative section).
- Confusables: Schein's three models of consultation (purchase of expertise or expert, doctor–patient, process consultation), which overlap with Block's expert role but are a different list; ATD/Robinson's "transactional vs. performance/strategic" consulting (see 3.2.1 below); "order taker" in TD writing is close to pair-of-hands.
- Variants or contested points: [S7] mentions Schein near the roles; the roles are normally attributed to Block, and Schein's own list is different. Do not credit the three roles to Schein.
- Unverified: the role definitions are paraphrased from general knowledge of the book; [S7] and [S8] confirm the three names only.

### Block's definition of a consultant and the three skill areas
- Origin: Block, *Flawless Consulting*, ch. 1 (4th ed.). [S7]
- Tie to the exam: 3.2 overall (an internal TD partner is a consultant in Block's sense).
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: definition plus a set of three
- Definition: a consultant is someone in a position to have some influence over a person, group or organization but with no direct power to make changes or implement programs. [S7] "no direct power to make changes or implement programs" Managers, by contrast, have direct control. TD staff advising line managers are consultants under this definition, even internally.
- Skill areas:
  1. Technical Skills: expertise in the content area (for TD: learning, performance, etc.). [S7] "Technical Skills"
  2. Interpersonal Skills: working with people (listening, support, disagreeing reasonably). [S7] "Interpersonal Skills"
  3. Consulting Skills: managing each phase of the consulting process (contracting, discovery, feedback, etc.). [S7] "Consulting Skills"
- Confusables: Domain 1 communication/influence models (`inform-influence`, `cialdini`); "influence without authority".
- Unverified: the one-line meanings of the three skills are general knowledge; names confirmed in [S7]. Block's two-part meaning of "flawless" (being authentic, and completing the business of each phase) is widely quoted but was not seen in the excerpt.

## 3.2.5 (3.2.A): Recommendations and stakeholder buy-in

### Stakeholder analysis steps
- Origin: Mind Tools' three-step method [S12], built around Mendelow's grid. General project-management practice; not an ATD-specific list.
- Tie to the exam: 3.2.5 (buy-in from stakeholders); also 3.2.1. Owner per OWNERSHIP.md: `biz`. `data` refers for 3.7.3 (stakeholder needs to frame analysis); `market` refers for communication planning.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: ordered (first to last)
- Items, in the source's terms [S12]:
  1. Identify your stakeholders: brainstorm everyone affected by the work, with power over it, or with an interest in its outcome.
  2. Prioritize your stakeholders: map them on a power/interest grid.
  3. Understand your key stakeholders: learn how they feel about the project and how best to engage and communicate with them.
- Confusables: Domain 1 `raci` (assigns roles on tasks; not a power/interest map); Domain 1 `strategic-message` and `value-prop`; ONA and informal power mapping (owner `od`, 3.3.4); needs assessment (APTD 3.2.4, not a CPTD statement).
- Variants or contested points: PMI/PMBOK uses a longer cycle (identify, plan engagement, manage engagement, monitor engagement); other sources add a step to plan communication. Don't treat "three steps" as an ATD rule.
- Unverified: no ATD or TDBoK source for the step list.

### Mendelow's power/interest grid (stakeholder map)
- Origin: A. L. Mendelow, 1981, "Environmental Scanning: The Impact of the Stakeholder Concept" (ICIS proceedings), as adapted in [S12].
- Tie to the exam: 3.2.5 and 3.2.1 ("stakeholder analysis and mapping" is assigned to `biz` in OWNERSHIP.md). Not on ATD's reading list; not shown in TDBoK text.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set (a 2×2 grid; axes are **power** over the work and **interest** in it)
- Items, quadrant → action, in the source's terms [S12]:
  1. High power, high interest → Manage Closely: fully engage these people and make the greatest effort to satisfy them. [S12] "fully engage these people"
  2. High power, less interested → Keep Satisfied: enough work to keep them satisfied, without over-communicating. [S12] "put enough work in with these people to keep them satisfied"
  3. Low power, high interest → Keep Informed: adequately inform them and check that no major issues are arising. [S12] "adequately inform these people"
  4. Low power, less interested → Monitor: watch them, but don't overwhelm them with excessive communication. [S12] "Monitor"
- Confusables: "Keep Satisfied" vs "Keep Informed" (the most common swap: high-power/low-interest is Satisfied; low-power/high-interest is Informed); the influence/impact grid and the salience model (power, legitimacy, urgency; Mitchell, Agle and Wood 1997; not researched here); SWOT's 2×2 (owner `strategy`); Domain 1 `raci`.
- Variants or contested points: (a) **date**: Mind Tools cites Mendelow 1981 [S12]; many sources cite Mendelow 1991 (a later ICIS paper). Avoid testing the year. (b) Some versions label the axes "influence" and "interest", or the bottom-left action "Monitor (minimum effort)". (c) Some sources call the high/high quadrant "Key players" (Johnson and Scholes's version of the matrix). Use the four actions above.
- Unverified: Mendelow's original wording (paper not read).

## 3.2.8 (3.2.D): Organizational barriers

Statement verbs, in order: identifying, minimizing, and overcoming; object: "organizational barriers to implementing talent development solutions and/or strategies" [S1].

### Six barriers to performance that learning does not address (ATD Blog, 2018)
- Origin: ATD Blog article, June 13, 2018, no author shown [S14]. It cites Rummler and Brache's *Improving Performance* (1995) in its work-systems section and ATD's April 2018 research report *Organizational Performance Improvement* [S14].
- Tie to the exam: 3.2.8 (organizational barriers to TD solutions working). Not on the reading list; ATD-published.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set (the article numbers them 1–6; no ranking claimed)
- Items, in the source's terms [S14]:
  1. Clarity of Roles and Expectations: people can't perform if they don't know what is expected of them. [S14] "People cannot perform successfully if they don't understand what is expected of them."
  2. Coaching and Reinforcement: without it, skills from learning don't transfer to the job; the article calls this the number one reason skills don't transfer. [S14] "The number one reason that skills developed through learning solutions do not transfer"
  3. Incentives: pay, plus meaningful work and other intangible rewards. [S14] "incentives go beyond just financial rewards."
  4. Work Systems and Processes: systems must support the desired way of working. [S14] "If you pit a good employee against a bad system, the system will win almost every time." (this section is where the article cites Rummler and Brache)
  5. Access to Information, People, Tools, and Job Aids: the right resources at the right time, not just more. [S14] "the right resources at the right time"
  6. Supportive Culture: behavior that conflicts with the prevailing culture is unlikely to stick. [S14] "that's not how we do things around here."
- How to minimize and overcome them (the article's advice) [S14]: look for root causes before choosing learning as the solution; partner with areas outside TD to remove barriers; expect several barriers at once, needing several solutions; when implementing learning, identify and address the other barriers too; treat the six as organizational capabilities the system must provide. The article cites ATD's 2018 report as finding 60% of respondents rated coaching the most effective performance solution.
- Confusables: **Robinson and Robinson's work-environment ("external") factors** in *Performance Consulting* (owner `perf`), which appear to share items 1–5 almost word for word (general knowledge, unverified here); Gilbert's BEM environment row (data/information, resources/instruments, incentives; owner `perf`); Mager–Pipe (`perf`); barriers to communication (Domain 1); 3.4.5's "anticipated constraints or problems ... (for example, resource deficiencies, lack of support)" (owner `strategy`), which is about anticipating constraints in planning rather than overcoming barriers in implementation; resistance to change (owner `change`).
- Variants or contested points: the six-item list with "Supportive Culture" is this article's; Robinson's list (if it is the source) is usually five work-environment factors plus internal factors (skill/knowledge, inherent capability). Don't merge them.
- Unverified: the author of [S14] (not shown; the list's wording suggests Robinson but that is a guess); the overlap with Robinson's list.
