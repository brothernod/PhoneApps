## 3.3.3: Theories and frameworks of social, organizational and informational systems

The statement names five examples, in this order: systems thinking, open systems theory, chaos and complexity theory, network theory, action research [S1]. Each needs at least one model below.

### Action research (cycle)
- Origin: Kurt Lewin, who coined "action research" in the 1940s (usually cited as "Action Research and Minority Problems", *Journal of Social Issues*, 1946). The eight-step OD version below is from Cummings and Worley (2009), via [S5]. Lewin attribution is general knowledge (not stated on [S5]).
- Tie to the exam: 3.3.3, named in the statement [S1]. [S5] calls it "the methodological basis for many planned models of organisational change".
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: ordered (an iterative cycle; the last step loops back to feedback)
- Items, in the source's own terms [S5] (steps quoted exactly):
  1. Problem identification: a key executive or someone with power senses a problem that action research might help with.
  2. Consultation with expert facilitator(s): client and OD practitioner (behavioral-science expert) share assumptions and values.
  3. Data collection and preliminary analysis: interviews, observation, questionnaires, performance data.
  4. Feedback to client/group: the data are fed back to the client or key group.
  5. Joint diagnosis of problem: client and practitioner diagnose together.
  6. Joint action planning: agree on the actions (interventions) to take.
  7. Action: carry out the changes.
  8. "Data gathering and action" as printed on [S5]; in Cummings and Worley the step is usually "data gathering after action" (collect data to evaluate the effects), which "can cycle back to step 4". [S5]
- Groups: the defining features are collaboration between practitioner and client ("joint"), data-driven diagnosis, and the cycle repeating (action, evaluation, new diagnosis).
- Confusables: (a) the ATD book's five OD phases (Entry, Diagnosis, Feedback, Solution, Evaluation) [S4], an OD process with similar steps; (b) Lewin's change model, unfreeze–change–refreeze (owner `change`: see change), by the same author; Cummings and Worley present action research, Lewin's change model and positive models (appreciative inquiry, owner `change`) as the three classic planned-change theories (memory, not on [S5]); (c) Lewin's leadership styles (`lewin`, Domain 1); (d) action learning (Revans/Marquardt; Domain 2 `people`): a group solving real problems and learning, not a research-and-change cycle.
- Variants or contested points: the four-step spiral often taught in education research (plan, act, observe, reflect; Kemmis and McTaggart) and Lewin's own "planning, action, fact-finding about the result of the action" are shorter versions of the same idea. Step counts differ (four, five or eight) by source. Which version ATD uses is not known; the TDBoK text was not available.
- Unverified: Lewin's 1946 paper and his own step wording (not fetched); the exact name of step 8 in Cummings and Worley (the [S5] wording looks like a slip).

### Open systems theory
- Origin: general systems theory, L. von Bertalanffy (1950s; *General System Theory*, 1968); applied to organizations by D. Katz and R. L. Kahn, *The Social Psychology of Organizations*, 1966 (2nd ed. 1978). General knowledge; no source fetched this session.
- Tie to the exam: 3.3.3, named in the statement [S1].
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: ordered for the core flow (input → throughput → output → feedback); the properties are a set
- Items (core flow, standard terms):
  1. Inputs: resources taken in from the environment (people, materials, money, information, energy).
  2. Throughput (transformation): the work processes that turn inputs into outputs.
  3. Outputs: products, services and results sent back into the environment.
  4. Feedback: information about outputs and the environment that returns as input and lets the system adjust.
  - Plus the environment itself and a permeable boundary: an open system depends on and exchanges with its environment; a closed system is treated as self-contained.
- Groups (Katz and Kahn's characteristics of open systems, as usually listed): importation of energy; throughput; output; systems as cycles of events; negative entropy; information input, negative feedback and the coding process; steady state and dynamic homeostasis; differentiation; equifinality. Key definitions: negative entropy = importing more energy than is used, so the system survives; equifinality = the same end state can be reached from different starting points and by different paths (so there is no one best way to organize).
- Confusables: closed-system views (scientific management, classic bureaucracy); systems thinking / `senge`; the BEM or Rummler's levels (owner `perf`); SIPOC; the Burke-Litwin and Weisbord diagnostic models, which are built on an open-systems view (not researched here).
- Variants or contested points: the count of Katz and Kahn's characteristics is usually given as nine (1966) or ten (1978, adding integration and coordination); this count is from memory and should not be tested.
- Unverified: everything in this entry (no tier 1–3 source fetched). Priority for gap fill because the statement names it.

### Chaos and complexity theory (complex adaptive systems)
- Origin: chaos theory from mathematics and meteorology (E. Lorenz, 1960s; the "butterfly effect"); complexity theory and complex adaptive systems from the Santa Fe Institute (1980s on); brought to management by writers such as M. Wheatley (*Leadership and the New Science*, 1992) and R. Stacey. General knowledge; not fetched.
- Tie to the exam: 3.3.3, named in the statement as one example, "chaos and complexity theory" [S1].
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: set (defining properties)
- Items (standard concepts):
  1. Nonlinearity: small causes can have large effects and large causes small ones; effects are not proportional.
  2. Sensitive dependence on initial conditions ("butterfly effect"): tiny differences at the start lead to very different outcomes, so long-range prediction fails.
  3. Emergence: system-wide patterns arise from local interactions among many agents, not from central design.
  4. Self-organization: order forms without a central controller.
  5. Attractors / patterns within chaos: chaotic systems are not random; behavior stays within recognizable bounds ("strange attractors").
  6. Edge of chaos: the zone between rigid order and chaos where adaptation and creativity are highest.
  7. Adaptation and co-evolution: agents learn and change in response to each other and the environment.
- Implications for TD/OD (common teaching points): plan less, experiment more; use small probes and fast feedback; distribute decision making; set simple rules and a clear purpose rather than detailed control.
- Confusables: Cynefin (below), which uses "complex" and "chaotic" as two different domains; open systems theory (both reject closed, mechanistic models); VUCA.
- Variants or contested points: chaos theory (deterministic but unpredictable systems) and complexity theory (many adaptive agents, emergence) are distinct in science; the outline treats them as one example.
- Unverified: all of it (general knowledge). Do not attribute specific property lists to a named author without a source.

### Cynefin framework
- Origin: D. Snowden (IBM, 1999); D. Snowden and M. Boone, "A Leader's Framework for Decision Making", *Harvard Business Review*, November 2007. General knowledge; not fetched.
- Tie to the exam: 3.3.3 (complexity theory applied to decisions). Not named in the statement or the reading list.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set of domains, each with an ordered decision pattern
- Items (2007 HBR names; response patterns):
  1. Simple (later "Obvious", now "Clear"): cause and effect obvious; best practice; sense–categorize–respond.
  2. Complicated: cause and effect knowable by analysis or experts; good practice; sense–analyze–respond.
  3. Complex: cause and effect seen only in hindsight; emergent practice; probe–sense–respond.
  4. Chaotic: no perceivable cause and effect; novel practice; act–sense–respond.
  5. Disorder (now "Confusion"; the center): not knowing which domain applies.
- Confusables: chaos and complexity theory (above); "complicated" vs "complex" is the classic trap.
- Variants or contested points: domain names changed over time (Simple → Obvious → Clear; Disorder → Confusion). Use the 2007 HBR names or give both.
- Unverified: all of it. Low priority; include only if an author wants a complexity application question.

### Network theory (social network concepts)
- Origin: social network analysis (sociometry, J. Moreno, 1930s); M. Granovetter, "The Strength of Weak Ties", *American Journal of Sociology*, 1973; R. Burt, *Structural Holes*, 1992. General knowledge; not fetched.
- Tie to the exam: 3.3.3, named in the statement [S1]; also 3.3.4 (informal relationships and power).
- Suggested tag: named
- Kind: set (terms)
- Items (standard terms):
  1. Nodes (actors): the people, teams or units in the network.
  2. Ties (links, edges): the relationships between them; can be strong or weak, one-way or mutual.
  3. Centrality: how important a node's position is. Common types: degree (number of direct ties), betweenness (how often a node sits on the paths between others: a broker or bottleneck), closeness (how quickly a node can reach everyone).
  4. Density: the share of possible ties that actually exist.
  5. Strength of weak ties (Granovetter): weak ties (acquaintances) bridge to other groups and bring new information; strong ties bring trust and support.
  6. Structural holes and brokerage (Burt): gaps between groups; people who bridge them gain information and influence.
  7. Clusters/silos and bridges: tightly linked subgroups and the ties that connect them.
- Confusables: organizational network analysis (the applied method, below); communities of practice (Domain 2 `techkm`, if present); French and Raven bases (power from position vs from network position).
- Unverified: all of it (general knowledge). The Granovetter and Burt citations are well known but were not fetched.

### Organizational network analysis (ONA)
- Origin: R. Cross and colleagues: R. Cross and L. Prusak, "The People Who Make Organizations Go—or Stop", *Harvard Business Review*, June 2002; R. Cross and A. Parker, *The Hidden Power of Social Networks*, HBR Press, 2004. General knowledge; not fetched.
- Tie to the exam: 3.3.3 (network theory applied) and 3.3.4 (identifying informal relationships and power). OWNERSHIP assigns it here.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set (roles); the method is ordered
- Items, roles from Cross and Prusak (2002):
  1. Central connectors: link most people in an informal network to one another; often not the formal leaders.
  2. Boundary spanners: connect the network to other parts of the company or to outside networks.
  3. Information brokers: link different subgroups; without them the network splits into silos.
  4. Peripheral specialists: experts at the edge of the network whom others consult when needed.
- Method (general): (1) set the business question; (2) collect relationship data (a survey such as "Whom do you go to for information or advice?", or passive data such as email and meeting metadata, with privacy safeguards); (3) map the network visually; (4) analyze (central people, bottlenecks, isolated people, silos, overloaded connectors); (5) act (connect groups, relieve bottlenecks, retain key connectors); (6) re-measure.
- Confusables: org chart (formal structure); Krackhardt and Hanson's advice/trust/communication networks (above); stakeholder mapping (owner `biz`).
- Variants or contested points: role names vary between Cross's later works (e.g. "brokers", "energizers"). Use the 2002 four if testing.
- Unverified: all of it (from memory). Gap fill should confirm the four role names against Cross and Prusak (2002).

### French and Raven's bases of power
- Origin: J. R. P. French and B. H. Raven, "The Bases of Social Power", in D. Cartwright (ed.), *Studies in Social Power*, 1959 (five bases); Raven, "Social influence and power", 1965 (added the sixth, informational). [S7]
- Tie to the exam: 3.3.4 ("power dynamics"). Not named in the statement and not seen on the reading list; it is the standard power taxonomy in OD and management texts. The OWNERSHIP file names it for this shard.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set (no order; often listed in the 1959 order below)
- Items, in the source's own terms [S6], [S7]:
  1. Coercive (coercion): power from the ability to punish or penalize. [S6] "the ability to penalize others"
  2. Reward: power from the ability to give benefits people want. [S6]
  3. Legitimate (legitimacy): power from a formal position, title or rank that others accept as a right to ask. [S6]
  4. Referent: power from being liked, admired or identified with (personality, charm). [S6]
  5. Expert: power from knowledge or skill others need. [S6]
  6. Informational (Raven 1965): power from controlling or using information to persuade; the only base that is "socially independent" (the change lasts because the target is convinced, not because of the source). [S7]
- Groups: Raven's own refinement [S7]: coercion and reward can each be personal or impersonal; expert and referent can be positive or negative; legitimacy splits into formal (position power) and normative forms (reciprocity, equity, responsibility/dependence); information can be direct or indirect. Many management texts also group the bases into positional/formal (legitimate, reward, coercive) and personal (expert, referent); [S7] notes informational "may be used in both personal and positional classifications". The positional/personal split is a textbook convention, not French and Raven's own.
- Confusables: Cialdini's principles of influence (`cialdini`, Domain 1): influence techniques, not power bases ("authority" and "liking" there are not "legitimate" and "referent" power); influence tactics (soft vs hard tactics, which [S7] cites to Forsyth 2010, not Raven); stakeholder power/interest grids (owner `biz`: see biz).
- Variants or contested points: **count.** The 1959 paper has five bases; Raven added informational in 1965, so "six bases" is also correct (Raven's own 2004 encyclopedia entry is titled "Power, Six Bases of" [S7]). Popular sources often say "five bases" and some add a seventh, "connection power" [S6] (Hersey and Goldsmith's later addition, not French and Raven's; attribution general knowledge). [S6] calls the sixth "information power"; Raven's term is "informational". Which count ATD uses: unknown; the TDBoK text was not available. Safe questions: ask for the five original bases, or say "Raven later added …", and never offer "connection" as a French and Raven base.
- Unverified: the 1959 and 1965 originals were not fetched (citations from [S7]); the Hersey and Goldsmith attribution of connection power.

### Formal vs informal organization (informal networks behind the chart)
- Origin: the distinction goes back to the Hawthorne studies and C. Barnard (1938); the best-known practitioner version is D. Krackhardt and J. R. Hanson, "Informal Networks: The Company Behind the Chart", *Harvard Business Review*, July–August 1993. General knowledge; not fetched this session.
- Tie to the exam: 3.3.4 names "formal and informal relationships". The ATD book's ch. 2 has "Other Ways to Work in or Around the Organization Chart" [S4].
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set
- Items: 1. Formal organization: the designed structure, as shown on the org chart (reporting lines, roles, official hierarchy, policies). 2. Informal organization: the actual relationships and networks through which work, information, influence and trust flow. Krackhardt and Hanson name three informal networks: (a) advice network (who people go to for help solving problems), (b) trust network (who shares sensitive information and backs whom in a crisis), (c) communication network (who talks to whom about work regularly).
- Confusables: organizational network analysis (below), the tool used to map the informal organization; APTD 3.3.6 (reporting structures, formal only).
- Unverified: all Krackhardt and Hanson details (network names) are from memory. Confirm before using the three-network list in a question.

### Schein's three levels of organizational culture
- Origin: E. H. Schein, *Organizational Culture and Leadership* (1st ed. 1985; 5th ed. 2016/2017 with P. A. Schein, Wiley). [S9] lists the 5th edition; the 1985 date is general knowledge.
- Tie to the exam: 3.3.8 (creating a culture). OWNERSHIP names culture models for this shard. Not seen on the reading list.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: ordered (most visible to deepest)
- Items [S9]:
  1. Artifacts (Wikipedia: "Artifacts and behaviours"): what you can see, hear and feel: architecture, office layout, dress code, jokes, rituals. Easy to observe, hard to interpret. [S9]
  2. Espoused beliefs and values (Wikipedia: "Espoused values"): the stated values, rules, philosophies and public statements. [S9] Trouble comes when they don't match the deeper assumptions.
  3. Basic underlying assumptions (Wikipedia: "Shared basic assumptions"): the unconscious, taken-for-granted beliefs that are the essence of culture and hard to see from inside. [S9]
- Confusables: Hofstede's national culture dimensions (national, not organizational culture; Domain 1 if present); the Competing Values Framework (below); the ATD book's values stages (3.3.2 above).
- Variants or contested points: level names vary by edition and summary (artifacts / espoused beliefs and values / basic underlying assumptions in later editions; "espoused values" and "shared basic assumptions" in [S9]). Use the three core words (artifacts, espoused values, assumptions) and accept either long form. Schein's definition of culture ("a pattern of shared basic assumptions" learned in solving problems of "external adaptation and internal integration") is general knowledge; [S9] does not contain it.
- Unverified: Schein's own wording of the level names and the definition (the book was not fetched).

### Competing Values Framework (four culture types)
- Origin: R. Quinn and J. Rohrbaugh (1983); K. Cameron and R. Quinn, *Diagnosing and Changing Organizational Culture* (1999; 3rd ed. 2011), with the Organizational Culture Assessment Instrument (OCAI). General knowledge; not fetched.
- Tie to the exam: 3.3.8 (culture). Not named in the statement or the reading list.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set (a 2x2)
- Items (dimensions: flexibility and discretion vs stability and control; internal focus and integration vs external focus and differentiation):
  1. Clan (collaborate): internal + flexible; family-like, mentoring, teamwork, participation.
  2. Adhocracy (create): external + flexible; entrepreneurial, innovation, risk-taking.
  3. Market (compete): external + stable; results, goals, competition, winning.
  4. Hierarchy (control): internal + stable; structure, rules, efficiency, consistency.
- Groups: OCAI rates six dimensions of culture: dominant characteristics, organizational leadership, management of employees, organizational glue, strategic emphases, criteria of success (memory).
- Confusables: Schein's levels (layers of one culture, not types); Deal and Kennedy culture types; "Clan" vs "Hierarchy" both internal.
- Unverified: all of it (from memory). Confirm quadrant placement before writing any question.

### Psychological safety (Edmondson; Kahn)
- Origin: W. A. Kahn (1990), at the individual level (one of his three conditions for engagement) [S10]; A. C. Edmondson, "Psychological Safety and Learning Behavior in Work Teams", *Administrative Science Quarterly*, 1999, at the team level; Edmondson, *The Fearless Organization*, Wiley, 2019. Edmondson details are general knowledge; not fetched.
- Tie to the exam: 3.3.8 (a culture where people speak up and give feedback). Not named in the statement or the reading list.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set (definition plus Edmondson's leader toolkit, which is ordered)
- Items:
  1. Kahn's definition: feeling able to show and employ oneself "without fear of negative consequences to self-image, status, or career" [S10]. Sources of it in Kahn: supportive, trusting relationships; group and intergroup dynamics; a supportive, consistent, non-punitive management style; clear norms [S10].
  2. Edmondson's definition (memory): a shared belief held by a team that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking.
  3. Edmondson's leader toolkit (2019, memory), in order: Setting the stage (frame the work, emphasize purpose); Inviting participation (situational humility, proactive inquiry, structures for input); Responding productively (express appreciation, destigmatize failure, sanction clear violations).
  4. Edmondson's four zones (memory), psychological safety x performance standards: Apathy (low/low), Comfort (high safety, low standards), Anxiety (low safety, high standards), Learning (high/high).
- Confusables: trust; Kahn's "safety" (individual) vs Edmondson's (team, shared); "psychological safety" is not the same as lowering standards (the Comfort zone).
- Unverified: all Edmondson content (definition, toolkit, zones), from memory. Kahn's part is supported by [S10].

### Dialogue (Bohm; Isaacs)
- Origin: D. Bohm, *On Dialogue* (1996); W. Isaacs, *Dialogue and the Art of Thinking Together*, 1999. Also Senge's team learning discipline (dialogue vs discussion), see `senge`. General knowledge; not fetched.
- Tie to the exam: 3.3.8 ("opportunities for dialogue and feedback").
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set
- Items (Isaacs's four practices for dialogue, memory):
  1. Listening: hearing without resistance or imposing your view.
  2. Respecting: treating others' views as legitimate.
  3. Suspending: holding your assumptions and judgments up for examination instead of defending them.
  4. Voicing: speaking your own authentic view.
- Groups: dialogue vs discussion (Bohm, Senge): discussion is about choosing and winning (ideas "batted back and forth"); dialogue explores meaning together and suspends assumptions.
- Confusables: `senge` (team learning); active listening models (Domain 1); the ATD book's Cycle of Active Listening (below).
- Unverified: all of it (from memory). Confirm the four practice names before testing.

### Gallup Q12
- Origin: Gallup (research by Buckingham and Coffman, *First, Break All the Rules*, 1999, and Wagner and Harter, *12: The Elements of Great Managing*, 2006; book attribution is general knowledge). Owner page [S8].
- Tie to the exam: 3.3.11 (assessing engagement). Not named in the statement or the reading list; it is the best-known engagement measure. OWNERSHIP names it for this shard.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: ordered (Gallup numbers the items Q01–Q12 and calls the four levels a hierarchy that builds from the base up) [S8]
- Items, Gallup's order (wording shortened from [S8]; the page prints them in reverse):
  1. Q01: I know what is expected of me at work.
  2. Q02: I have the materials and equipment I need to do my work right.
  3. Q03: I have the opportunity to do what I do best every day.
  4. Q04: In the last seven days, I have received recognition or praise for good work.
  5. Q05: My supervisor, or someone at work, seems to care about me as a person.
  6. Q06: There is someone at work who encourages my development.
  7. Q07: At work, my opinions seem to count.
  8. Q08: The mission or purpose of my company makes me feel my job is important.
  9. Q09: My associates (fellow employees) are committed to doing quality work.
  10. Q10: I have a best friend at work. [S8] "I have a best friend at work."
  11. Q11: In the last six months, someone at work has talked to me about my progress.
  12. Q12: In the last year, I have had opportunities at work to learn and grow.
  - Plus an overall satisfaction item ("How satisfied are you with your company as a place to work?"), often labeled Q00; it is not one of the 12. [S8]
- Groups: Gallup's four-level hierarchy, base to top: Basic Needs, Individual Contribution, Teamwork, Growth [S8]. Item mapping (general knowledge, not on [S8]): Basic Needs = Q01–Q02; Individual Contribution = Q03–Q06; Teamwork = Q07–Q10; Growth = Q11–Q12. (Buckingham and Coffman's version asks "What do I get?", "What do I give?", "Do I belong?", "How can we all grow?" for the four levels; memory.)
- Confusables: Maslow's hierarchy (`maslow`, Domain 1; Gallup's pyramid is often compared to it); employee satisfaction (the Q00 item) vs engagement; Gallup's three employee categories: engaged, not engaged, actively disengaged (general knowledge; not on [S8]).
- Variants or contested points: some summaries call level 2 "Management Support" instead of "Individual Contribution"; [S8] uses "Individual Contribution". Item wording varies a little between sources; Gallup's exact item wording is copyrighted, so questions should paraphrase. The 1–5 agreement scale was not shown on [S8].

### Kahn's personal engagement (three psychological conditions)
- Origin: W. A. Kahn, "Psychological conditions of personal engagement and disengagement at work", *Academy of Management Journal* 33(4), 692–724, 1990 [S10]. Usually cited as the first academic theory of work engagement (general knowledge).
- Tie to the exam: 3.3.11 / 3.3.12 (what engagement is and what drives it). Not named in the statement or the reading list.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set
- Items [S10]:
  - Personal engagement: "employing and expressing the self" in a work role, physically, cognitively and emotionally (the three dimensions). [S10]
  - Personal disengagement: withdrawing and defending the self from the role. [S10]
  - Three psychological conditions:
    1. Meaningfulness: whether it feels worthwhile to bring oneself into the role. [S10]
    2. Safety: whether it feels safe to do so, without fear of negative consequences to self-image, status or career. [S10]
    3. Availability: whether one has the physical, emotional and cognitive resources to engage at that moment. [S10]
- Confusables: Gallup Q12 (a measure, not a theory); UWES's vigor/dedication/absorption (also three parts, different names); Edmondson's team psychological safety (3.3.8).
- Unverified: Kahn's often-quoted definition "the harnessing of organization members' selves to their work roles" (not on [S10]); the influences on meaningfulness and availability (task, role and work interactions; physical and emotional energy, insecurity, outside life), from memory.

### Utrecht Work Engagement Scale (UWES): vigor, dedication, absorption
- Origin: W. Schaufeli, M. Salanova, V. González-Romá and A. Bakker, *Journal of Happiness Studies*, 2002; UWES manual (Schaufeli and Bakker, 2003/2004). General knowledge; not fetched.
- Tie to the exam: 3.3.11 (measuring engagement). Not named in the statement or the reading list.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set
- Items: engagement as a positive, fulfilling, work-related state of mind with three parts:
  1. Vigor: high energy and mental resilience while working; persistence.
  2. Dedication: strong involvement; sense of significance, enthusiasm, pride, challenge.
  3. Absorption: full concentration; time passes quickly; hard to detach.
- Variants or contested points: long (17-item) and short (9-item) versions exist; often framed as the opposite of burnout (Maslach's exhaustion, cynicism, inefficacy).
- Confusables: Kahn's physical/cognitive/emotional; Gallup Q12.
- Unverified: all of it (from memory).

### Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) model
- Origin: E. Demerouti, A. Bakker, F. Nachreiner and W. Schaufeli, 2001; A. Bakker and E. Demerouti, 2007. General knowledge; not fetched.
- Tie to the exam: 3.3.12 (engagement drivers: what to change in work design). Not named in the statement or the reading list.
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set (two categories, two processes)
- Items:
  1. Job demands: aspects of work that need sustained effort and have costs (workload, time pressure, emotional demands); drive the health-impairment process toward strain and burnout.
  2. Job resources: aspects that help reach goals, reduce demands or support growth (autonomy, support, feedback, development opportunities); drive the motivational process toward engagement.
  - Later versions add personal resources (self-efficacy, optimism) and job crafting.
- Confusables: Herzberg's two-factor theory (hygiene vs motivators; Domain 1 if present).
- Unverified: all of it (from memory). Low priority.

### Engagement measurement methods and metrics
- Origin: common practice; no single source. eNPS adapts Reichheld's Net Promoter Score (Bain, 2003); Say/Stay/Strive is Aon Hewitt's engagement model. General knowledge; not fetched.
- Tie to the exam: 3.3.11 (assessing and evaluating engagement). Survey analysis belongs to `data` (see data); questionnaire design is Domain 2 (`questionnaire-items`).
- Suggested tag: extra
- Kind: set (options)
- Items:
  1. Annual (census) engagement survey: the full survey of all employees, benchmarked over time or against norms (e.g. Gallup Q12).
  2. Pulse surveys: short, frequent surveys on a few items to track trends.
  3. Employee Net Promoter Score (eNPS): "How likely are you to recommend this organization as a place to work?" on 0–10; promoters 9–10, passives 7–8, detractors 0–6; eNPS = % promoters minus % detractors (range −100 to +100).
  4. Focus groups and interviews (including stay interviews with current employees and exit interviews with leavers) to explain the survey numbers.
  5. Outcome and behavioral indicators: voluntary turnover and retention, absenteeism, productivity, quality, safety incidents, customer satisfaction, internal mobility, participation rates.
  6. Say / Stay / Strive (Aon Hewitt): engaged employees speak positively about the organization (Say), intend to stay (Stay), and give extra effort (Strive).
- Process (common practice): measure → share results → action-plan with managers and teams → communicate what changed ("close the loop", cf. the ATD book's listening cycle) → re-measure. Not acting on survey results is widely cited as reducing future participation and engagement.
- Confusables: satisfaction (contentment) vs engagement (energy and commitment) vs organizational commitment (Meyer and Allen: affective, continuance, normative; memory).
- Unverified: all of it (eNPS bands and formula, Say/Stay/Strive names, Meyer and Allen components), from memory. The eNPS bands follow the standard NPS bands.
